Exercise: hierarchical models

Goal

By the end of this exercise you should be able to:

  • tell a biological replicate from a technical one, and count the effective sample size of a design,
  • recognise pseudoreplication in a described analysis and say what goes wrong,
  • compute a partial-pooling (shrinkage) estimate by hand from the precision-weighted-average formula,
  • read the two variance components (\(\sigma^2\) within, \(\tau^2\) between) and turn them into an ICC,
  • write the R model formula y ~ x + (1 | group) and say what each part means in both lme4 and brms.
Warning

This Bayesian statistics block is provisional and may move to a later statistics / ML course. Focus on the concepts (replicates, pooling, shrinkage) — the by-hand shrinkage calculation is the part most likely to survive into any version.

Background

Grouped data have (at least) two levels of variation. For measurement \(j\) in group \(i\):

\[ y_{ij} = \mu + b_i + \varepsilon_{ij}, \qquad b_i \sim \mathcal N(0,\tau^2), \quad \varepsilon_{ij} \sim \mathcal N(0,\sigma^2). \]

  • \(\mu\) — overall mean (fixed effect).
  • \(b_i\) — group \(i\)’s deviation (random effect), a draw from a shared \(\mathcal N(0,\tau^2)\).
  • \(\sigma^2\) — within-group (technical / measurement) variance.
  • \(\tau^2\) — between-group (biological) variance.

Partial pooling. The estimate for a group is a precision-weighted average of its own mean \(\bar y_i\) (from \(n_i\) measurements) and the grand mean \(\hat\mu\):

\[ \hat\mu_i = w_i\,\bar y_i + (1-w_i)\,\hat\mu, \qquad w_i = \frac{n_i/\sigma^2}{\,n_i/\sigma^2 + 1/\tau^2\,}. \]

The pull of \(\hat\mu_i\) toward \(\hat\mu\) is shrinkage; it is larger when \(n_i\) is small or \(\tau^2\) is small.

Variance partition. The intraclass correlation

\[ \text{ICC} = \frac{\tau^2}{\tau^2 + \sigma^2} \]

is the fraction of variance that is between groups — how much your hierarchy matters.

By hand

B1. A study reports: “We measured a protein in 4 mice, taking 50 sections per mouse (n = 200), and found a significant difference between genotypes (p < 0.001).” For the between-genotype question, what is the relevant number of independent units? Name the error being made.

B2. For each design, say whether the extra measurements are biological or technical replicates: (a) the same blood sample split into three tubes and each assayed once; (b) three separately recruited patients, each assayed once; (c) one cell line grown, then split and sequenced in three lanes; (d) three independently grown cultures of the same cell line, each sequenced once.

B3. A group has \(n_i = 4\) measurements with sample mean \(\bar y_i = 10\). The grand mean is \(\hat\mu = 6\), the within-group variance is \(\sigma^2 = 8\), and the between-group variance is \(\tau^2 = 2\). (a) Compute the weight \(w_i\). (b) Compute the partial-pooling estimate \(\hat\mu_i\). (c) In one sentence, why is \(\hat\mu_i\) between 6 and 10?

B4. Using the same \(\sigma^2 = 8\), \(\tau^2 = 2\), \(\hat\mu = 6\), now consider a group with only \(n_i = 1\) measurement whose single value is \(\bar y_i = 10\). Compute \(w_i\) and \(\hat\mu_i\). Compared with B3, does this group shrink more or less toward the grand mean? Explain in terms of \(n_i\).

B5. With \(\sigma^2 = 8\) and \(\tau^2 = 2\), compute the ICC. Interpret it: if two measurements come from the same group, how correlated are they, and does grouping matter much here?

B6. Take the partial-pooling weight \(w_i = \dfrac{n_i/\sigma^2}{n_i/\sigma^2 + 1/\tau^2}\). Show algebraically that: (a) as \(\tau^2 \to 0\), \(w_i \to 0\) (which pooling regime is this?); (b) as \(\tau^2 \to \infty\), \(w_i \to 1\) (which pooling regime is this?).

B7. (design) You have budget for 40 assays and want to compare treated vs control. You can spend it as 4 patients × 10 measurements or 20 patients × 2 measurements. If the ICC is high (patients differ a lot, within-patient noise is small), which design gives you more power for the between-patient comparison, and why?

Explore

The demo below simulates eight patients with \(n_i\) from 1 to 20 measurements, using the numbers of B3–B5: \(\sigma^2 = 8\), true \(\tau^2 = 2\), overall mean 6. Open dots are raw patient means \(\bar y_i\), filled dots the partial-pooling estimates \(\hat\mu_i\), the dashed line the grand mean \(\hat\mu\), and the green tick the true patient mean (known only because the data are simulated). The weight \(w_i\) is printed on the right. Do B3–B4 by hand first.

E1. Click true τ² (\(\tau^2 = 2\)). Read the weight of the \(n = 1\) patient. Does it match your B4 answer? Which patients would have the weight \(0.5\) of B3?

E2. Drag \(\tau^2\) to 0 and then to \(\infty\). Describe where the filled dots go in each case and match each end to a pooling regime (B6).

E3. Look at the total error² vs truth box and the red curve. Which of no pooling, complete pooling and partial pooling at the true \(\tau^2\) has the smallest total error? Press new data five times: is the winner always the same?

E4. With \(\tau^2 = 2\), find a patient whose filled dot is further from its green tick than its open dot is. How can shrinkage make a single patient worse and still be a good idea?

Solutions

E1. \(w = 0.20\) for the \(n = 1\) patient, as in B4. A weight of \(0.5\) needs \(n_i/\sigma^2 = 1/\tau^2\), i.e. \(n_i = 4\); no patient here has exactly 4 measurements, so the \(n = 3\) and \(n = 5\) patients bracket it (\(w = 0.43\) and \(0.56\)).

E2. At \(\tau^2 = 0\) all weights are 0 and every filled dot sits on the grand mean: complete pooling. At \(\tau^2 = \infty\) all weights are 1 and every filled dot sits on its open dot: no pooling. In between, small-\(n\) patients move most.

E3. On the first data set partial pooling has the smallest total error (about 13 vs 17 for complete and 23 for no pooling). Over repeated data sets partial pooling usually wins, but not every time: on some data sets complete pooling or no pooling happens to do better. Shrinkage lowers the error on average.

E4. A patient whose true mean really is far from the grand mean gets pulled toward the centre, away from its truth. Shrinkage trades a little bias for individual extreme groups against a large reduction in noise for small groups; summed over all patients, the error is usually smaller.

In R

R is only for confirming the by-hand answers and seeing the model syntax. Nothing here is executed at build time (no packages are installed), so treat these as templates to run in your own session.

Confirm a shrinkage calculation (base R, no packages)

# B3 by hand, reproduced in R
n_i    <- 4
ybar_i <- 10
mu_hat <- 6
sigma2 <- 8
tau2   <- 2

w    <- (n_i / sigma2) / (n_i / sigma2 + 1 / tau2)
mu_i <- w * ybar_i + (1 - w) * mu_hat
c(weight = w, estimate = mu_i)     # compare against your hand answer

The ICC

sigma2 <- 8
tau2   <- 2
icc    <- tau2 / (tau2 + sigma2)
icc                                # fraction of variance that is between-group

Fitting the model (templates — run in your own session)

# Frequentist multilevel model
library(lme4)
set.seed(1)
d <- data.frame(patient = factor(rep(seq_len(40), each = 4)),
                treatment = rep(c("control", "control", "treated", "treated"), 40))
patient_effect <- rnorm(40, sd = sqrt(2))
d$biomarker <- 6 + 1.5 * (d$treatment == "treated") +
  patient_effect[as.integer(d$patient)] + rnorm(nrow(d), sd = sqrt(8))
fit <- lmer(biomarker ~ treatment + (1 | patient), data = d)
summary(fit)     # fixed effect + variance components (tau^2, sigma^2)
ranef(fit)       # the shrunken per-patient deviations b_i
# Bayesian version — identical formula
library(brms)
fit <- brm(biomarker ~ treatment + (1 | patient),
           data = d, family = gaussian())
summary(fit)     # posterior means + credible intervals

Tasks

R1. Reproduce B3 and B4 with the base-R shrinkage snippet (change only n_i). Confirm the \(n_i = 1\) group shrinks more.

R2. Compute the ICC for \(\sigma^2 = 8, \tau^2 = 2\) (matches B5), then re-compute it for \(\tau^2 = 32\). Which scenario makes grouping matter more?

R3. Write, without running, the lmer/brm formula for a design with cells nested in patients where you want a treatment fixed effect. (Hint below.)

Hints

Hint 1 — biological vs technical

Ask: does this measurement come from a new, independent biological unit, or from the same unit measured again? A new unit = biological replicate (lets you generalise). Same unit remeasured = technical replicate (only reduces measurement noise). See the replicate discussion in the lecture and the ideas of independent sampling from the Statistics and data science for biologists course.

Hint 2 — pseudoreplication (B1)

The number that limits a between-group claim is the number of independent groups, not the total count of measurements. Counting technical/within-unit measurements as if they were independent biological units is pseudoreplication; it makes standard errors too small and p-values too optimistic.

Hint 3 — the shrinkage formula (B3, B4)

Plug into \(w_i = \dfrac{n_i/\sigma^2}{n_i/\sigma^2 + 1/\tau^2}\), then \(\hat\mu_i = w_i\bar y_i + (1-w_i)\hat\mu\). Compute the two “precisions” first: \(n_i/\sigma^2\) (how much the group knows about itself) and \(1/\tau^2\) (how tightly groups cluster). The weight is the group’s precision as a fraction of the total.

Hint 4 — why smaller n shrinks more (B4)

\(w_i\) increases with \(n_i\): more of its own measurements → the group trusts its own mean more → less shrinkage. With \(n_i = 1\) the group carries little information, so it leans on the grand mean.

Hint 5 — ICC (B5)

\(\text{ICC} = \dfrac{\tau^2}{\tau^2+\sigma^2}\) is a fraction between 0 and 1. It is both the fraction of total variance that is between-group and the correlation between two measurements from the same group. Near 1 → grouping dominates; near 0 → groups are interchangeable.

Hint 6 — the limits (B6)

Substitute the extreme value of \(\tau^2\) into the weight. For \(\tau^2 \to 0\), the term \(1/\tau^2 \to \infty\) dominates the denominator, so \(w_i \to 0\). For \(\tau^2 \to \infty\), \(1/\tau^2 \to 0\) vanishes, leaving \(w_i \to (n_i/\sigma^2)/(n_i/\sigma^2) = 1\). Match each to complete vs no pooling.

Hint 7 — R formulas

In lme4::lmer and brms::brm the syntax is identical. (1 | group) = a random intercept per level of group. Nesting is written (1 | patient/cell) (equivalently (1 | patient) + (1 | patient:cell)); crossed factors are separate terms (1 | batch) + (1 | patient). Fixed effects (the ones you care about, with few chosen levels) go on the left, e.g. treatment.

Solutions

Solution

B1. The between-genotype comparison has 4 independent units (the 4 mice), not 200. The 50 sections per mouse are technical / within-unit replicates: they make each mouse’s value more precise but do not add independent biological units. Analysing the 200 sections as if independent is pseudoreplication; it understates the standard error and produces a p-value that is too small. The honest design has \(n = 4\) for the genotype question.

B2. (a) Technical — one sample split into three; same biological unit measured three times. (b) Biological — three independently recruited patients; three independent units. (c) Technical — one culture split across lanes; the lanes are sequencing (measurement) replicates of the same unit. (d) Biological — three independently grown cultures; the independent growth is what makes them biological replicates.

B3. (a) Precisions: group precision \(n_i/\sigma^2 = 4/8 = 0.5\); between-group precision \(1/\tau^2 = 1/2 = 0.5\). \[w_i = \frac{0.5}{0.5 + 0.5} = \frac{0.5}{1.0} = 0.5.\] (b) \[\hat\mu_i = 0.5\cdot 10 + (1-0.5)\cdot 6 = 5 + 3 = 8.\] (c) \(\hat\mu_i = 8\) lies between the group’s own mean (10) and the grand mean (6) because the estimate is a compromise: the group’s 4 measurements pull toward 10, the shared distribution of groups pulls toward 6, and here the two carry equal weight.

B4. Now \(n_i = 1\): group precision \(n_i/\sigma^2 = 1/8 = 0.125\); between-group precision still \(1/\tau^2 = 0.5\). \[w_i = \frac{0.125}{0.125 + 0.5} = \frac{0.125}{0.625} = 0.2.\] \[\hat\mu_i = 0.2\cdot 10 + 0.8\cdot 6 = 2 + 4.8 = 6.8.\] This group shrinks more toward the grand mean (6.8 is much closer to 6 than B3’s 8 was). Reason: with only \(n_i = 1\) measurement the group carries little information about itself (\(w_i = 0.2 < 0.5\)), so the estimate leans heavily on the group of groups.

B5. \[\text{ICC} = \frac{\tau^2}{\tau^2 + \sigma^2} = \frac{2}{2 + 8} = \frac{2}{10} = 0.2.\] Two measurements from the same group are correlated \(0.2\). Only 20% of the total variance is between groups, so grouping matters but is not dominant here — most variation is within groups (measurement/within-unit noise).

B6. (a) As \(\tau^2 \to 0\): \(1/\tau^2 \to \infty\), so the denominator \(n_i/\sigma^2 + 1/\tau^2 \to \infty\) while the numerator \(n_i/\sigma^2\) stays finite, giving \(w_i \to 0\). Then \(\hat\mu_i \to \hat\mu\) for every group — complete pooling (groups treated as identical). (b) As \(\tau^2 \to \infty\): \(1/\tau^2 \to 0\), so \(w_i \to \dfrac{n_i/\sigma^2}{n_i/\sigma^2 + 0} = 1\). Then \(\hat\mu_i \to \bar y_i\) — no pooling (each group estimated on its own).

B7. With a high ICC, most of the variance is between patients and within-patient measurements are highly redundant (each extra one adds little new information). The between-patient comparison is limited by the number of patients, so 20 patients × 2 measurements gives far more power than 4 patients × 10 measurements. Ten measurements on only 4 patients pins down each patient’s mean precisely but still leaves you with just 4 independent units for the treatment question. General lesson: when ICC is high, add groups, not measurements per group.

R1. Base-R reproduction:

shrink <- function(n_i, ybar_i, mu_hat = 6, sigma2 = 8, tau2 = 2) {
  w <- (n_i / sigma2) / (n_i / sigma2 + 1 / tau2)
  c(weight = w, estimate = w * ybar_i + (1 - w) * mu_hat)
}
shrink(n_i = 4, ybar_i = 10)   # weight 0.5,  estimate 8.0   (B3)
shrink(n_i = 1, ybar_i = 10)   # weight 0.2,  estimate 6.8   (B4)

The \(n_i = 1\) group has the smaller weight (0.2 vs 0.5) and lands closer to the grand mean — more shrinkage, confirming B4.

R2.

icc <- function(sigma2, tau2) tau2 / (tau2 + sigma2)
icc(sigma2 = 8, tau2 = 2)    # 0.2   (B5)
icc(sigma2 = 8, tau2 = 32)   # 0.8

\(\tau^2 = 32\) gives ICC \(= 0.8\): now 80% of variance is between groups, so grouping matters much more and the effective sample size is close to the number of groups.

R3. Cells nested in patients, treatment as a fixed effect:

lmer(biomarker ~ treatment + (1 | patient/cell), data = d)
# equivalently: biomarker ~ treatment + (1 | patient) + (1 | patient:cell)
# Bayesian: brm(biomarker ~ treatment + (1 | patient/cell), data = d)

treatment is the fixed effect of interest; (1 | patient/cell) gives a random intercept for each patient and for each cell within a patient.

Where this leads

  • Penalized linear models: shrinkage here and ridge/lasso penalties there are the same idea from two angles — reining in extreme estimates. A random-effect variance \(\tau^2\) plays the role of a penalty strength that the data choose for you.
  • Prior, likelihood, posterior (this block): the line \(b_i \sim \mathcal N(0,\tau^2)\) is a prior on the group effects, with \(\tau\) estimated from the data — an adaptive prior. Hierarchical modelling is Bayesian inference with an extra layer.
  • Error models (Poisson / negative binomial): real omics counts replace the Gaussian \(\varepsilon_{ij}\) with a count distribution; the hierarchical structure carries over unchanged, and overdispersion is itself a between-unit variance.
