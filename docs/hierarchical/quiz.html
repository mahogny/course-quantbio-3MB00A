Quiz: hierarchical models

Short questions to test your understanding of hierarchical (multilevel) models: replicates, pooling, shrinkage, and the random-effects formulation. Try each on paper first, then open the answer. Several are in the style of the on-campus exam (no computer).

Warning

This Bayesian statistics block is provisional and may move to a later statistics / ML course.

Q1. In one sentence, what is the difference between a biological replicate and a technical replicate?

Hint

One gives you a new independent unit; the other remeasures a unit you already had.

Answer

A biological replicate is an independent biological unit (a different patient, animal, or independently grown culture) and captures between-unit variation; a technical replicate is the same unit measured again (a split sample, a re-sequenced library) and captures only measurement noise.

Q2. A paper reports “n = 300 cells from 3 mice, p < 0.001” for a difference between two mouse strains. What is the relevant sample size for the between-strain claim, and what is this error called?

Hint

Which number limits your ability to generalise to new mice?

Answer

The relevant sample size is 3 (the mice), not 300. Treating the 300 cells as independent units is pseudoreplication; it overstates the effective sample size, so standard errors are too small and the p-value is too optimistic.

Q3. Name the three ways to handle grouped data and say, in a few words, what each assumes.

Hint

Two are extremes (between-group variance of 0 or infinity); one is the middle.

Answer

Complete pooling — one mean for everyone (assumes groups are identical, \(\tau^2 = 0\)). No pooling — a separate independent mean per group (assumes groups tell you nothing about each other, \(\tau^2 = \infty\)). Partial pooling — each group estimate is a compromise between its own mean and the grand mean (\(\tau^2\) estimated from the data); this is the hierarchical model.

Q4. In partial pooling, which groups shrink most toward the grand mean, and why?

Hint

Look at how the weight \(w_i = \dfrac{n_i/\sigma^2}{n_i/\sigma^2 + 1/\tau^2}\) depends on \(n_i\).

Answer

Groups with few (or noisy) measurements shrink most. A small \(n_i\) makes the group’s own precision \(n_i/\sigma^2\) small, so the weight \(w_i\) on its own mean is small and it leans on the grand mean. Data-rich groups have \(w_i\) near 1 and barely move.

Q5. A group has \(n_i = 3\) measurements with mean \(\bar y_i = 12\). The grand mean is \(\hat\mu = 6\), \(\sigma^2 = 6\), \(\tau^2 = 1\). Compute the partial-pooling estimate \(\hat\mu_i\).

Hint

Compute \(n_i/\sigma^2\) and \(1/\tau^2\), form \(w_i\), then \(\hat\mu_i = w_i\bar y_i + (1-w_i)\hat\mu\).

Answer

\(n_i/\sigma^2 = 3/6 = 0.5\); \(1/\tau^2 = 1\). So \(w_i = \dfrac{0.5}{0.5 + 1} = \dfrac{0.5}{1.5} = \tfrac13\). Then \(\hat\mu_i = \tfrac13\cdot 12 + \tfrac23\cdot 6 = 4 + 4 = 8\). The estimate (8) sits between the group mean (12) and the grand mean (6), closer to 6 because the group carries relatively little information.

Q6. Write the random-effects model \(y_{ij} = \mu + b_i + \varepsilon_{ij}\) and say which term is the fixed effect, which is the random effect, and what the two variances mean.

Hint

One term is a single number for the whole dataset; one is a draw per group.

Answer

\(\mu\) is the fixed effect (overall mean, one number). \(b_i \sim \mathcal N(0,\tau^2)\) is the random effect (group \(i\)’s deviation, a draw from a shared distribution), with between-group variance \(\tau^2\). \(\varepsilon_{ij} \sim \mathcal N(0,\sigma^2)\) is the residual with within-group (measurement) variance \(\sigma^2\).

Q7. The intraclass correlation is \(\text{ICC} = \tau^2/(\tau^2+\sigma^2)\). If \(\tau^2 = 9\) and \(\sigma^2 = 1\), compute it and say what it means for your effective sample size.

Hint

ICC near 1 means measurements within a group are almost redundant.

Answer

\(\text{ICC} = 9/(9+1) = 0.9\). Most variance is between groups and within-group measurements are highly correlated (redundant), so your effective sample size is close to the number of groups, not the total number of measurements. To gain power you need more groups, not more measurements per group.

Q8. What does the R formula y ~ treatment + (1 | patient) specify, and is it different in lme4 versus brms?

Hint

Left of the bar = what varies by group; right of the bar = the grouping factor.

Answer

It specifies a fixed effect of treatment (the population-level question) plus a random intercept (1 | patient) — one deviation \(b_i\) per patient drawn from a shared \(\mathcal N(0,\tau^2)\). The formula is identical in lme4::lmer (frequentist) and brms::brm (Bayesian, via Stan); brms additionally places priors on all parameters and returns a full posterior.

Q9. From the Bayesian viewpoint, what is a random effect?

Hint

Look at the line \(b_i \sim \mathcal N(0,\tau^2)\) — what role does it play?

Answer

A random effect is a prior on the group-level parameters: \(b_i \sim \mathcal N(0,\tau^2)\) is a prior on the group deviations, and because its width \(\tau\) is estimated from the data it is an adaptive (hierarchical) prior. Partial pooling is exactly the posterior compromise between each group’s data and this learned prior.
