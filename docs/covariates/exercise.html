Exercise: handling covariates

Goal

By the end of this exercise you should be able to:

  • write a model formula for a research question and say what each term does,
  • turn a formula into a design matrix \(X\) by hand, for both continuous and categorical predictors,
  • read off what each coefficient means, including the crucial “holding the other predictors constant,”
  • explain confounding and why adjusting for a covariate can change — even reverse — an apparent effect,
  • interpret an interaction term (\(a * b\)) and compute a subgroup-specific effect,
  • recognise the common pitfalls (wrong reference level, collinearity, over-adjustment).

By-hand design-matrix and coefficient interpretation are the examined skills; the R section only confirms your answers.

Background

A model formula is a recipe for a design matrix. The model is

\[ \mathbf{y} = X\boldsymbol{\beta} + \boldsymbol{\varepsilon}, \qquad \hat y_i = \beta_0 + \beta_1 x_{i1} + \beta_2 x_{i2} + \dots \]

Building the columns of \(X\):

  1. Intercept — a column of all 1’s (present by default). Its coefficient \(\beta_0\) is the predicted outcome when every other predictor is 0 (for factors: at the reference level).
  2. Continuous predictor (age, BMI) — the raw numbers, one column. Coefficient = change in \(\hat y\) per unit.
  3. Categorical predictor (a factor with \(k\) levels) — \(k-1\) dummy (0/1) columns under the default treatment contrasts. The omitted level is the reference, folded into the intercept; each dummy’s coefficient = that level minus the reference.

Adjustment. Adding + z gives \(z\) its own column, so the other coefficients are now interpreted holding \(z\) constant. A confounder (a common cause of the predictor of interest and the outcome) must be adjusted for, or the effect you report is biased.

Interaction. a * b expands to a + b + a:b. The product term a:b lets the effect of a depend on the level of b. Under treatment contrasts, the main effect of a is the effect of a at the reference level of b, and the interaction is the extra effect at the other level.

Reference levels. R orders factor levels alphabetically by default, so the alphabetically-first level is the reference. Change it with relevel or factor(x, levels = ...).

By hand

B1. Write model formulas (in y ~ ... form) for each question: (a) does expression depend on treatment alone? (b) does expression depend on treatment, adjusting for age? (c) does expression depend on treatment and sex, allowing the treatment effect to differ between the sexes?

B2. You have 4 samples:

sample treatment age
1 ctrl 10
2 treat 40
3 ctrl 22
4 treat 35

Write out the design matrix \(X\) for the formula expr ~ treatment + age by hand (treatment coding, ctrl as reference). Label the columns.

B3. For the fitted model expr ~ treatment + age you obtain \(\beta_0 = 5\), \(\beta_1 = 3\) (treatmenttreat), \(\beta_2 = 0.1\) (age). In words: (a) what is the predicted expression for a treated animal aged 30? (b) what is the treatment effect, and what does “holding age constant” add to that statement? (c) what does \(\beta_0 = 5\) correspond to, and why is it not very meaningful here?

B4. (categorical with three levels) A factor tissue has levels brain, gut, liver. For expr ~ tissue: (a) how many dummy columns does \(X\) have (besides the intercept)? (b) which level is the reference under the default coding? (c) if the intercept is 2 and the coefficient tissuegut is 4, what is the predicted mean expression in gut? In brain?

B5. (confounding) In a study, treated animals were mostly old and controls mostly young, and expression rises with age. The unadjusted model expr ~ treatment gives a large positive treatmenttreat. After fitting expr ~ treatment + age, the treatmenttreat coefficient shrinks toward 0. (a) explain, in terms of confounding, why it shrank; (b) which of the two estimates is the honest treatment effect, and why?

B6. (interaction) For expr ~ treatment * sex with references ctrl and F, you fit: \[\beta_0 = 5,\quad \beta_1\,(\text{treat}) = 2,\quad \beta_2\,(\text{M}) = 1,\quad \beta_3\,(\text{treat:M}) = 3.\] (a) what is the treatment effect in females? (b) what is the treatment effect in males? (c) what does \(\beta_3 = 3\) mean in words? What would \(\beta_3 = 0\) imply?

B7. (reference level) Your treatment factor has levels drug and placebo. You fit expr ~ treatment and get a coefficient named treatmentplacebo. (a) which level did R choose as the reference, and why? (b) the coefficient treatmentplacebo is \(-4\). Interpret it. Is the drug raising or lowering expression relative to placebo? (c) how would you make placebo the reference so the coefficient reads as the drug effect?

In R

Use R to confirm the by-hand answers. The models below are illustrative; the same formulas work in a Bayesian fit (rstanarm::stan_glm, brms::brm) with priors added.

See the design matrix a formula builds

treatment <- factor(c("ctrl","treat","ctrl","treat"))
age       <- c(10, 40, 22, 35)

model.matrix(~ treatment + age)
#   (Intercept) treatmenttreat age
# 1           1              0  10
# 2           1              1  40
# 3           1              0  22
# 4           1              1  35

Check the reference level of a factor

levels(treatment)                 # "ctrl" "treat"  -> ctrl is the reference
contrasts(treatment)              # shows the 0/1 coding

# force a different reference:
treatment2 <- relevel(treatment, ref = "treat")
levels(treatment2)                # "treat" "ctrl"

A factor with three levels

tissue <- factor(c("brain","gut","liver","gut"))
model.matrix(~ tissue)
#   (Intercept) tissuegut tissueliver
# 1           1         0           0     # brain = reference (all dummies 0)
# 2           1         1           0
# 3           1         0           1
# 4           1         1           0

An interaction expands into three terms

sex <- factor(c("F","M","F","M"))
colnames(model.matrix(~ treatment * sex))
# "(Intercept)" "treatmenttreat" "sexM" "treatmenttreat:sexM"

Tasks

R1. Reproduce the design matrix from B2 with model.matrix(~ treatment + age) and confirm it matches your hand answer column-for-column.

R2. For B4, build tissue <- factor(c("brain","gut","liver")) and run model.matrix(~ tissue). Confirm the number of dummy columns and which level is the reference.

R3. For B7, build treatment <- factor(c("drug","placebo","drug","placebo")), check levels(), then relevel() to make placebo the reference and re-check levels().

R4. Confirm the interaction expansion from B6: colnames(model.matrix(~ treatment * sex)) should list treatmenttreat, sexM, and the product treatmenttreat:sexM.

Hints

Hint 1 — reading a formula

+ means “also include this term as a predictor” (not arithmetic). y ~ a + b fits an additive model; y ~ a * b adds the interaction a:b on top of the two main effects. There is always an intercept unless you write y ~ 0 + ... or y ~ ... - 1.

Hint 2 — building the design matrix

Start with a column of 1’s (the intercept). Continuous predictors go in as their raw numbers. For a factor under the default treatment contrasts, add one 0/1 column per non-reference level: 1 when the sample is that level, 0 otherwise. Use model.matrix to check.

Hint 3 — what a coefficient means

Every coefficient is read “holding the other predictors constant.” For a continuous predictor it is the change in \(\hat y\) per unit. For a factor dummy it is the difference between that level and the reference. The intercept is \(\hat y\) when all predictors are 0 / at their reference levels.

Hint 4 — predicting from coefficients (B3)

Plug the sample’s values into \(\hat y = \beta_0 + \beta_1\cdot\text{treat} + \beta_2\cdot\text{age}\). A treated animal has treat = 1; a control has treat = 0. So a treated animal aged 30 gives \(\hat y = 5 + 3(1) + 0.1(30)\).

Hint 5 — confounding (B5)

A confounder is associated with both the group and the outcome. The unadjusted treatment coefficient captures the real treatment effect plus the age difference between groups. Adding age to the model lets the age effect be estimated separately, so the treatment coefficient keeps only what is left over — the effect “at the same age.”

Hint 6 — interaction coefficients (B6)

Under treatment coding the main effect \(\beta_1\) is the treatment effect in the reference sex (F). The interaction \(\beta_3\) is the extra treatment effect in males, so the male treatment effect is \(\beta_1 + \beta_3\). If \(\beta_3 = 0\) the effect is identical in both sexes (no interaction).

Hint 7 — reference levels (B7)

R orders factor levels alphabetically, so drug (before placebo) becomes the reference and the reported coefficient is treatmentplacebo = placebo − drug. To read it as the drug effect instead, make placebo the reference with relevel(treatment, ref = "placebo").

Solutions

Solution

B1. (a) expr ~ treatment (b) expr ~ treatment + age (c) expr ~ treatment * sex (equivalently expr ~ treatment + sex + treatment:sex). The * allows the treatment effect to differ between sexes via the interaction term.

B2. Intercept column of 1’s; a single treatment dummy (treatmenttreat = 1 for treated, 0 for control, since ctrl is the reference); and the raw age column.

\[ X = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 & 10 \\ 1 & 1 & 40 \\ 1 & 0 & 22 \\ 1 & 1 & 35 \end{pmatrix} \quad \begin{array}{l} \text{col 1: (Intercept)} \\ \text{col 2: treatmenttreat} \\ \text{col 3: age} \end{array} \]

B3. (a) \(\hat y = \beta_0 + \beta_1\cdot 1 + \beta_2\cdot 30 = 5 + 3 + 0.1\times30 = 5 + 3 + 3 = 11\). (b) The treatment effect is \(\beta_1 = 3\): treated animals have expression 3 units higher than controls of the same age. “Holding age constant” makes clear this is not contaminated by any age imbalance between the groups — it is the treatment effect after age has been accounted for. (c) \(\beta_0 = 5\) is the predicted expression for a control animal of age 0. Age 0 is outside any real data (and biologically a newborn / extrapolation), so the raw intercept is not scientifically meaningful. Centring age (I(age - mean(age))) would move the intercept to the mean age and make it interpretable.

B4. (a) Three levels → \(3 - 1 = 2\) dummy columns (tissuegut, tissueliver), plus the intercept. (b) brain — it is alphabetically first, so it is the reference (all its dummies are 0). (c) Gut mean \(=\) intercept \(+\) tissuegut \(= 2 + 4 = 6\). Brain mean \(=\) intercept \(= 2\) (brain is the reference, so both dummies are 0).

B5. (a) Age is a confounder: it differs between the groups (treated older, controls younger) and affects the outcome (expression rises with age). In expr ~ treatment the treatment coefficient absorbs the treatment effect plus the age-driven difference between the groups, inflating it. Adding + age gives age its own coefficient, so the age contribution is removed from the treatment coefficient, and it shrinks toward the true value. (b) The adjusted estimate (expr ~ treatment + age) is the honest one: it estimates the treatment effect among animals of the same age, disentangling it from the age imbalance. The unadjusted estimate conflates the two.

B6. (a) Treatment effect in females (the reference sex): \(\beta_1 = 2\). (b) Treatment effect in males: \(\beta_1 + \beta_3 = 2 + 3 = 5\). (c) \(\beta_3 = 3\) is the extra treatment effect in males compared with females — the treatment raises expression 3 units more in males than in females (a difference of treatment effects). \(\beta_3 = 0\) would mean the treatment acts equally in both sexes, i.e. no interaction, and the additive model expr ~ treatment + sex would suffice.

B7. (a) drug — factor levels are ordered alphabetically, and drug comes before placebo, so drug is the reference; the non-reference dummy is treatmentplacebo. (b) treatmentplacebo \(= -4\) means placebo expression is 4 units lower than drug. Equivalently, the drug raises expression by 4 units relative to placebo. (The sign is about placebo-minus-drug because drug is the reference.) (c) treatment <- relevel(treatment, ref = "placebo") (or factor(treatment, levels = c("placebo","drug"))). Then the coefficient becomes treatmentdrug \(= +4\), directly the drug effect.

R1–R4. These confirm the hand answers.

treatment <- factor(c("ctrl","treat","ctrl","treat"))
age       <- c(10, 40, 22, 35)
model.matrix(~ treatment + age)     # R1: matches the X in B2

tissue <- factor(c("brain","gut","liver"))
model.matrix(~ tissue)              # R2: 2 dummies; brain is reference

treatment <- factor(c("drug","placebo","drug","placebo"))
levels(treatment)                   # R3: "drug" "placebo" -> drug is reference
treatment <- relevel(treatment, ref = "placebo")
levels(treatment)                   # "placebo" "drug"  -> placebo now reference

sex <- factor(c("F","M","F","M"))
colnames(model.matrix(~ treatment * sex))   # R4: main effects + product term

Where this leads

  • Error models (Poisson / negative binomial): for count outcomes (RNA-seq), the covariates still enter through the design matrix \(X\boldsymbol\beta\), but inside a log link (\(\log \mu = X\boldsymbol\beta\)) with a count likelihood instead of a Gaussian one.
  • Hierarchical models: when samples share structure (several technical replicates per animal, several animals per group), covariate effects can be allowed to vary across replicates — the distinction between biological and technical replicates becomes a modelling choice.
  • Penalized linear models: when you have many covariates or collinear ones, plain fitting becomes unstable (an identifiability problem); L1/L2 penalties shrink the coefficients to tame it.
  • Design and contrast matrices recur in every differential-expression tool (limma, DESeq2, edgeR): the model.matrix() you built here is exactly the design = argument those tools expect.
