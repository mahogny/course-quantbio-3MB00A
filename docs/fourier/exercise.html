Exercise: Fourier transform & basis functions

Goal

By the end of this exercise you should be able to:

  • describe a sinusoid by its frequency, amplitude, and phase, and read those off a picture,
  • explain in your own words why the Fourier transform is a change of basis, and why it is reversible,
  • explain why sinusoids of different frequencies form an orthogonal basis, and how that lets each amplitude be read off as a projection,
  • contrast the fixed Fourier basis with the data-driven PCA basis, and say when each is appropriate,
  • reason about what a spectrum shows, and how low-pass / high-pass / notch filtering and compression act on it,
  • read a small fft() output in R and interpret the magnitude spectrum.

This is an Applications-block appreciation exercise. It builds conceptual fluency and light intuition — there is no by-hand transform to grind through. Do the reasoning parts on paper first, then use the short R section to confirm the picture.

Background

A sinusoid is one pure wave, \(A\cos(2\pi f\,t + \varphi)\), set by three numbers: frequency \(f\) (cycles per unit), amplitude \(A\) (height), phase \(\varphi\) (starting point in the cycle).

The Fourier transform writes any signal as a sum of sinusoids and returns the amplitude and phase of every frequency — the recipe of waves. Equivalently, it re-expresses the signal from the standard basis (one coordinate per time point or pixel) into the Fourier basis (one coordinate per frequency). It is a change of basis: the same object in new coordinates, fully reversible via the inverse transform.

The Fourier basis works because sinusoids of different frequencies are orthogonal — treating whole signals as vectors, \(\langle f,g\rangle = \sum_t f(t)g(t) = 0\) for two different-frequency waves. So each frequency’s amplitude is a projection of the signal onto that wave, computed independently of the others — the same projection idea as reading off PCA scores.

Two families of use:

  • Compression keeps the few large (usually low-frequency) coefficients and discards the rest — like truncating a PCA to its top components (JPEG uses the DCT, a Fourier relative).
  • Filtering multiplies the spectrum by a mask and transforms back: low-pass smooths/denoises, high-pass sharpens, a notch removes one artefact frequency. This is fast because of the convolution theorem (convolution in space = multiplication in frequency).

Fixed vs learned basis: Fourier commits to sinusoids for every signal; PCA learns axes from the data’s covariance.

By hand (reasoning)

B1. Sketch (roughly) three sinusoids over one unit of time: (a) \(\cos(2\pi\cdot 1\cdot t)\), (b) \(\cos(2\pi\cdot 4\cdot t)\), (c) \(2\cos(2\pi\cdot 1\cdot t)\). How many cycles does each complete? Which pair differs only in amplitude, and which only in frequency?

B2. A signal is described as “mostly a slow smooth trend with a little fine texture on top.” Where does its energy sit in the spectrum — low frequencies, high frequencies, or both? Which part would a low-pass filter keep?

B3. Explain, in one or two sentences each, why the Fourier transform is (a) a change of basis and (b) reversible (no information lost).

B4. Two people record the same biological signal but one starts the recording a fraction of a second later. Which of the three sinusoid parameters — frequency, amplitude, phase — is most affected, and which are essentially unchanged? What does this tell you about why the magnitude spectrum (amplitudes only) is often more useful than the phase?

B5. For each task, say whether a fixed Fourier basis or a data-driven PCA basis is the more natural first choice, and why: (a) detecting a regular striation pattern in a muscle micrograph; (b) finding the main axes of variation in a gene-expression table (cells × genes); (c) removing a 50 Hz electrical hum from an electrophysiology trace; (d) summarising a large set of noisy spectra into a few components that capture most of the variance.

B6. A microscopy image has a faint, perfectly regular diagonal stripe artefact from the detector. In the 2D spectrum this appears as an isolated pair of bright dots. Describe how you would remove the artefact using the frequency domain, and name the filter type. What real structure might you accidentally damage, and how does that connect to the honesty caveat from the lecture?

B7. “Noise is high-frequency, so I’ll just delete all high frequencies and my image will be clean.” Give one reason this works reasonably well and one reason it can go wrong.

B8. (compression) An image’s Fourier coefficients are dominated by a handful of large low-frequency values; the rest are tiny. Explain the compression recipe in three steps, and state the analogy to truncating a PCA. Why is this lossy, and what artefact appears if you keep too few coefficients?

Explore: filter an image

The demo below is the lecture’s micrograph (a 256 × 256 crop). Left: the image; middle: its 2D spectrum, log of the magnitude, with frequency 0 in the centre; right: the image rebuilt from the coefficients you keep. Removed coefficients are shown dark red. The readout gives the share of coefficients kept, the share of the image’s variation they carry, and the RMS error of the rebuilt image (pixel values run from 0 to 1).

E1. Choose low-pass and set the radius to 5, 30 and 90. For each, note the percentage of coefficients kept and the percentage of the variation kept. What does that tell you about where the image’s content sits in the spectrum?

E2. Choose keep largest at 3%. Then go back to low-pass and find the radius that also keeps about 3% of the coefficients. Which of the two has the smaller error? Look at where the kept coefficients lie in the spectrum in “keep largest” mode: why are the two results so similar?

E3. Choose high-pass with radius 10. What is left of the cell? Why is the picture grey rather than black, although most of the low frequencies are gone?

E4. Tick add stripe artefact and choose no filter: note the error. The stripe adds two bright coefficients to the spectrum, marked with yellow rings. Erase them by dragging over them. What happens to the error? Why are there two dots for one stripe? Then try removing the stripe with a low-pass filter instead, at radius 15. Which approach is better here?

Solutions

E1. Radius 5 keeps 0.12% of the coefficients but already 53% of the variation; radius 30 keeps 4.3% of the coefficients and 81% of the variation; radius 90 keeps 39% and 96%. A tiny disc of low frequencies carries most of the variation: the broad light and dark regions. The many high-frequency coefficients each carry little; together they make the fine detail (and the grain). This is why a frequency basis compresses natural images well.

E2. Keep largest 3%: 81% of the variation, RMS error 0.100. Low-pass with radius 25 also keeps 3.0% of the coefficients: 78% of the variation, error 0.109. Keeping the largest coefficients is slightly better, because it picks the coefficients that matter for this image, while the disc is fixed in advance. They are similar because most of the largest coefficients are low frequencies anyway: in “keep largest” mode the kept coefficients crowd around the centre, with a scatter of single ones further out.

E3. Only edges and fine texture remain: the outline of the nucleus, the membranes, the grain inside the cell. Large uniform regions all become the same grey, because their brightness is low-frequency content. The picture stays grey because the centre coefficient (frequency 0, the average brightness of the whole image) is always kept; without it the values would vary around 0 and the image would be mostly black.

E4. With the stripe and no filter the error is 0.106 (the stripe itself). The stripe is a single 2D wave, so it adds a single bright pair of coefficients, about 19 units from the centre (16 across, 10 down, and its mirror image). Erasing them brings the error down to about 0.02; zeroing exactly the two coefficients would give 0.002, the brush also removes a few of the cell’s own coefficients around them. There are two dots because a real-valued wave is the sum of a frequency and its mirror image (\(+k\) and \(-k\)), the same mirror symmetry as in the 1D spectrum in R (R2 below). A low-pass filter at radius 15 also removes the stripe, but it removes the cell’s detail too: the error is 0.127, worse than leaving the stripe in. A notch removes exactly what you want to remove; a low-pass removes everything above a frequency.

In R

Use R only to confirm the intuition above. This is illustrative static code — read it and predict the output before you would run it.

Build a signal and look at its spectrum

# a signal: a slow 3-cycle wave + a smaller fast 20-cycle wave + noise
set.seed(1)
n <- 256
t <- (0:(n - 1)) / n  # n equally spaced samples; frequency k is FFT bin k
x <- sin(2*pi*3*t) + 0.5*sin(2*pi*20*t) + rnorm(n, sd = 0.2)

X   <- fft(x)          # discrete Fourier transform -> complex numbers
mag <- Mod(X)          # magnitude = amount of each frequency
mag <- mag[1:(n/2)]    # keep the lower half; the upper half mirrors it

plot(mag, type = "h", xlab = "frequency index", ylab = "amplitude")
# expect two clear spikes: a tall one at R index 4 (frequency 3), another at index 21 (frequency 20)

The transform is reversible

x_back <- Re(fft(X, inverse = TRUE) / length(X))   # inverse transform
max(abs(x - x_back))    # ~0 (tiny numerical error): the signal is recovered

A crude low-pass filter (denoising in the frequency domain)

X_lp <- X
keep <- 6                       # keep only the lowest few frequencies
X_lp[(keep+1):(n-keep+1)] <- 0  # zero out the high-frequency middle band
x_lp <- Re(fft(X_lp, inverse = TRUE) / n)
# x_lp is a smoothed version of x: the fast 20-cycle wave and the noise are gone

Tasks

R1. In the first block, before looking: at which two frequency indices do you expect spikes, and which should be taller? (Match the amplitudes in the formula for x.)

R2. Why do we take Mod(X) rather than plotting X directly, and why do we keep only the first half mag[1:(n/2)]? (Two short reasons — one about complex numbers, one about symmetry.)

R3. In the low-pass block, predict what happens to x_lp if you increase keep from 6 to 30. Will it look more like the smooth trend or more like the original noisy signal? Explain in terms of which frequencies you are now keeping.

R4. Explain why the reconstruction block divides by length(X). (Base R’s fft() is unnormalised, so the round-trip picks up a factor of \(n\).)

Hints

Hint 1 — reading a sinusoid (B1)

The number in front of \(t\) inside \(\cos(2\pi f\,t)\) is the frequency \(f\) = cycles per unit time, so over \(t\in[0,1]\) it completes exactly \(f\) cycles. The number multiplying the whole cosine is the amplitude (height). See the wave picture in the lecture, and R’s cos.

Hint 2 — low vs high frequency (B2, B7)

Low frequencies = slow, broad, smooth structure. High frequencies = fast, fine detail — sharp real edges and random noise both live here. That overlap is exactly why blunt high-frequency removal is imperfect.

Hint 3 — change of basis (B3)

A basis is a set of coordinate directions; changing basis re-expresses the same object in new coordinates. Standard basis: one coordinate per time point. Fourier basis: one coordinate per frequency. Reversible because the inverse transform exists — see fft with inverse = TRUE. Compare with the change-of-basis idea from Matrices as transformations and PCA.

Hint 4 — phase vs magnitude (B4)

Shifting when you start recording slides every wave along in its cycle — it changes the phase but leaves each wave’s frequency and amplitude intact. So the magnitude spectrum is invariant to a time shift, which is why amplitude-only summaries are often the robust choice.

Hint 5 — fixed vs data-driven basis (B5)

Ask: do I expect structure at particular frequencies (rhythms, stripes, hums) → Fourier, a fixed basis. Or do I want axes that adapt to my dataset’s variation (expression tables, sets of spectra) → PCA, a learned basis. Both are orthogonal bases read off by projection.

Hint 6 — removing an artefact (B6)

A perfectly periodic artefact is localised in frequency (a few bright dots), so you can zero out just those frequencies — a notch (band-stop) filter — and transform back. The risk: any real structure at that same frequency is deleted too.

Hint 7 — reading fft() output (R1–R4)

fft returns complex numbers; use Mod for amplitude and Arg for phase. For a real input the spectrum is mirror-symmetric, so only the first half is informative. The transform is unnormalised, so the inverse must be divided by the length. The spike height tracks the amplitude in the formula for x.

Solutions

Solution

B1. Over \(t\in[0,1]\): (a) \(\cos(2\pi\cdot1\cdot t)\) completes 1 cycle; (b) \(\cos(2\pi\cdot4\cdot t)\) completes 4 cycles; (c) \(2\cos(2\pi\cdot1\cdot t)\) completes 1 cycle but is twice as tall. So (a) and (c) differ only in amplitude (same frequency, different height); (a) and (b) differ only in frequency (same height, different number of cycles).

B2. A slow smooth trend is low-frequency; the fine texture on top is high-frequency. So the energy sits mostly at low frequencies with a small high-frequency contribution — both, but dominated by the low end. A low-pass filter keeps the low frequencies, i.e. it keeps the smooth trend and removes the fine texture.

B3. (a) Change of basis: the signal is one object; in the standard basis its coordinates are its values at each time point (one axis per time point), and the Fourier transform re-expresses the same object in the Fourier basis, where the coordinates are the amounts of each frequency (one axis per frequency). Nothing but the coordinate system changed. (b) Reversible: the Fourier basis is a complete orthogonal basis, so the transform is an invertible rotation of coordinates — the inverse Fourier transform reconstructs the original signal exactly, so no information is lost.

B4. Starting the recording later slides every constituent wave along in its cycle, which changes the phase of every frequency. The frequencies present and their amplitudes are unchanged (the same waves are there, equally strong). Therefore the magnitude spectrum (amplitudes only) is unaffected by when you started recording, while the phase spectrum is. That shift-invariance is a big reason amplitude-only summaries are often preferred for comparing signals.

B5. (a) Fourier — a regular striation is periodic, so it appears at a specific spatial frequency; a fixed sinusoid basis is the natural detector. (b) PCA — you want axes that adapt to the data’s own directions of variation; there is no reason expression variation should align with fixed sinusoids. (c) Fourier — a 50 Hz hum is a single frequency; a notch filter in the frequency domain removes it cleanly. (d) PCA — “capture most of the variance in a few components” is exactly what a learned, data-driven basis does.

B6. Transform the image to the 2D frequency domain, locate the isolated bright dots produced by the periodic stripe, set those coefficients (a small band around them) to zero — a notch / band-stop filter — then apply the inverse transform to get the cleaned image. Risk: if any genuine image structure happens to sit at that same spatial frequency, it is removed along with the artefact. This is the lecture’s honesty caveat — filtering in frequency space cannot distinguish “artefact at frequency \(f\)” from “real signal at frequency \(f\)”, so it is always a trade-off.

B7. Works reasonably well because random noise really is spread across high frequencies, so cutting them removes a lot of noise. Can go wrong because sharp real edges and fine true detail are also high-frequency, so a blunt cut blurs genuine structure along with the noise. A frequency mask cannot tell noise from real detail when both sit at the same frequencies, so filtering is always a trade-off between removing noise and keeping detail.

B8. Three-step compression recipe: (1) transform the image to the frequency domain; (2) keep only the largest / lowest-frequency coefficients and discard the many small ones; (3) transform back (or store the kept coefficients and reconstruct on demand). This is the same idea as truncating a PCA to its top components — represent the data in a smart basis, then keep only the coordinates that carry most of the signal. It is lossy because the discarded coefficients held some (small) real information; keep too few and you lose real high-frequency content, producing visible blocky / blurred artefacts (in JPEG, block boundaries become visible because the DCT is applied on small blocks).

R1. The spikes are at frequencies 3 and 20. In this one-based R vector they appear at indices 4 and 21, because index 1 is the zero-frequency (DC) term. The frequency-3 spike is taller, because that component has amplitude 1 versus 0.5 for the frequency-20 component. The noise contributes a low, roughly flat floor across all frequencies.

R2. We take Mod(X) because fft() returns complex numbers (real + imaginary parts encoding amplitude and phase); Mod() extracts the amplitude, which is what we want to see. We keep only mag[1:(n/2)] because for a real input signal the spectrum is mirror-symmetric (the upper half is a redundant reflection of the lower half), so the first half contains all the information.

R3. Raising keep from 6 to 30 keeps more of the higher frequencies. At keep = 6 you keep only the slowest components, so x_lp is a very smooth curve close to the 3-cycle trend. At keep = 30 you now also retain the 20-cycle component (and more of the noise band), so x_lp looks more like the original noisy signal — less smoothing. More kept frequencies = less aggressive low-pass = closer to the original.

R4. Base R’s fft() is unnormalised: fft() followed by fft(..., inverse = TRUE) returns the original signal multiplied by n = length(X). Dividing by length(X) cancels that factor so the reconstruction matches the original signal, up to tiny floating-point error.

Where this leads

  • PCA (this Applications block): the data-driven counterpart to Fourier — instead of fixing the basis to sinusoids, it learns the orthogonal axes from the data’s covariance. Both are “represent in an orthogonal basis, then keep the coordinates that matter.”
  • Polar coordinates: measuring a ring (next unit): Mod() and Arg() are the distance \(r\) and angle \(\theta\) of each coefficient; that unit uses the same polar coordinates on an image, as an example of a useful but nonlinear transformation.
  • Chemometrics: spectra (mass, IR, Raman) are signals in frequency/wavelength; Fourier methods, filtering, and PCA of spectra are everyday tools there.
