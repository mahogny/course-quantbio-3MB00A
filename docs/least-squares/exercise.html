Exercise: least squares as projection

Goal

By the end of this exercise you should be able to, by hand and in R:

  • write a line fit \(y = a + bx\) through a few points as an over-determined system \(X\boldsymbol\beta = \mathbf{y}\),
  • compute residuals and the sum of squared residuals for a given line,
  • check whether a fit is the least-squares fit, by testing that the residual is orthogonal to every column of \(X\),
  • set up and solve the normal equations \(X^\mathsf{T}X\boldsymbol\beta = X^\mathsf{T}\mathbf{y}\) for a small example,
  • confirm that solve(t(X) %*% X, t(X) %*% y) and coef(lm(y ~ x)) give the same answer,
  • recognise when \(\boldsymbol\beta\) is not unique.

Do the by-hand problems with pen and paper first, then check with R.

Background

Fitting a line \(y = a + bx\) to \(n\) points gives one equation per point. In matrix form, with \(\boldsymbol\beta = (a, b)\):

\[ X\boldsymbol\beta = \mathbf{y}, \qquad X = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & x_1 \\ \vdots & \vdots \\ 1 & x_n \end{pmatrix}, \qquad \mathbf{y} = \begin{pmatrix} y_1 \\ \vdots \\ y_n \end{pmatrix}. \]

Object Meaning
Columns of \(X\) \(\mathbf{1} = (1, \dots, 1)\) (intercept) and \(\mathbf{x}\) (the predictor)
Fitted values \(\hat{\mathbf{y}} = X\boldsymbol\beta\), a combination \(a\,\mathbf{1} + b\,\mathbf{x}\) of the columns
Residual \(\mathbf{r} = \mathbf{y} - X\boldsymbol\beta\)
Sum of squared residuals \(\lVert\mathbf{r}\rVert^2 = \mathbf{r}\cdot\mathbf{r} = \sum_i r_i^2\)
Least squares the \(\boldsymbol\beta\) with the smallest \(\lVert\mathbf{r}\rVert^2\)
Orthogonality test least squares \(\iff\) \(\mathbf{1}\cdot\mathbf{r} = 0\) and \(\mathbf{x}\cdot\mathbf{r} = 0\)
Normal equations \(X^\mathsf{T}X\boldsymbol\beta = X^\mathsf{T}\mathbf{y}\)

For a line, the normal equations are two equations in \(a\) and \(b\):

\[ X^\mathsf{T}X = \begin{pmatrix} \mathbf{1}\cdot\mathbf{1} & \mathbf{1}\cdot\mathbf{x} \\ \mathbf{x}\cdot\mathbf{1} & \mathbf{x}\cdot\mathbf{x} \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} n & \sum_i x_i \\ \sum_i x_i & \sum_i x_i^2 \end{pmatrix}, \qquad X^\mathsf{T}\mathbf{y} = \begin{pmatrix} \mathbf{1}\cdot\mathbf{y} \\ \mathbf{x}\cdot\mathbf{y} \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} \sum_i y_i \\ \sum_i x_i y_i \end{pmatrix}. \]

Geometrically: \(\hat{\mathbf{y}}\) is the projection of \(\mathbf{y}\) onto the plane spanned by the columns of \(X\), and \(\mathbf{r}\) is the orthogonal leftover, as in Vectors, dot product & projection.

By hand

A gene’s log2 expression in three samples taken at a drug dose of 0, 1 and 2 µM:

dose \(x\) 0 1 2
log2 expression \(y\) 1 3 2

B1. Write the three equations \(a + bx = y\), one per sample. Show that no line passes through all three points: solve the first two equations for \(a\) and \(b\), and check the third.

B2. Write the system as \(X\boldsymbol\beta = \mathbf{y}\): give the \(3 \times 2\) matrix \(X\) and the vector \(\mathbf{y}\).

B3. A colleague proposes the line \(y = 1 + x\) (it goes through the first two points).

  1. Compute the fitted values, the residual vector \(\mathbf{r}\), and the sum of squared residuals.
  2. Compute \(\mathbf{1}\cdot\mathbf{r}\) and \(\mathbf{x}\cdot\mathbf{r}\). Is this the least-squares line?

B4. Set up the normal equations: compute \(X^\mathsf{T}X\) and \(X^\mathsf{T}\mathbf{y}\), write the two equations in \(a\) and \(b\), and solve them.

B5. For your answer to B4:

  1. Compute the fitted values \(\hat{\mathbf{y}}\) and the residual \(\mathbf{r}\).
  2. Check that \(\mathbf{r}\) is orthogonal to both columns of \(X\).
  3. Compute the sum of squared residuals and compare it with B3a.

B6. Intercept only. Fit the model y ~ 1, i.e. \(X\) is the single column \(\mathbf{1} = (1, 1, 1)\), to the same \(\mathbf{y}\). Use the projection formula \(\hat{\mathbf{y}} = \dfrac{\mathbf{y}\cdot\mathbf{1}}{\mathbf{1}\cdot\mathbf{1}}\,\mathbf{1}\). What is the fitted constant, and what familiar number is it?

B7. Through the origin. Fit the model y ~ x - 1 (no intercept), i.e. \(X\) is the single column \(\mathbf{x} = (0, 1, 2)\). Use the same projection formula with \(\mathbf{x}\) in place of \(\mathbf{1}\). What is the slope? Is it the same as the slope in B4? Why not?

B8. A familiar system. In Linear systems in R, the over-determined system

\[ x = 2, \qquad y = 3, \qquad x + y = 6 \]

had least-squares solution \((x, y) = (2.33, 3.33)\) from qr.solve. Here \(x\) and \(y\) are the unknowns. Write the matrix \(A\), set up the normal equations \(A^\mathsf{T}A\begin{pmatrix} x \\ y \end{pmatrix} = A^\mathsf{T}\mathbf{b}\), and solve them by hand. Do you get the same answer?

B9. No unique answer. Suppose all three samples were taken at the same dose, \(\mathbf{x} = (1, 1, 1)\), with the same \(\mathbf{y} = (1, 3, 2)\).

  1. Compute \(X^\mathsf{T}X\). Can the normal equations be solved uniquely?
  2. Find two different pairs \((a, b)\) that give the same fitted values. What are those fitted values?

In R

Check your by-hand answers. Build \(X\) with cbind, transpose with t, and solve the normal equations with solve:

x <- c(0, 1, 2)
y <- c(1, 3, 2)
X <- cbind(1, x)          # columns: intercept, dose
X
model.matrix(~ x)         # the same matrix, built from a formula

# B3: the colleague's line y = 1 + x
r_bad <- y - X %*% c(1, 1)
r_bad
sum(r_bad^2)
t(X) %*% r_bad            # not zero -> not least squares

# B4: normal equations
t(X) %*% X
t(X) %*% y
beta <- solve(t(X) %*% X, t(X) %*% y)
beta

# B5: fitted values, residual, orthogonality
yhat <- X %*% beta
r    <- y - yhat
t(X) %*% r                # zero up to rounding
sum(r^2)

# the same with lm
fit <- lm(y ~ x)
coef(fit)
fitted(fit)
resid(fit)

R1. Run the block. Do beta and coef(fit) agree? Do fitted(fit) and resid(fit) match B5?

R2. Check B6 and B7 with coef(lm(y ~ 1)), mean(y), coef(lm(y ~ x - 1)) and sum(x * y) / sum(x * x).

R3. Check B8: build A and b and compare solve(t(A) %*% A, t(A) %*% b) with qr.solve(A, b).

R4. More points. Simulate a larger experiment and check that the normal equations still give lm’s answer:

set.seed(1)
dose <- rep(c(0, 0.5, 1, 2, 4), each = 4)          # 20 samples
expr <- 2 + 0.8 * dose + rnorm(20, sd = 0.5)       # true line: a = 2, b = 0.8
X <- cbind(1, dose)
solve(t(X) %*% X, t(X) %*% expr)
coef(lm(expr ~ dose))
plot(dose, expr, pch = 19)
abline(lm(expr ~ dose), col = "red")

How close are the estimates to the true \(a = 2\), \(b = 0.8\)? Compute t(X) %*% resid(lm(expr ~ dose)).

R5. Check B9: with x <- c(1, 1, 1), what do solve(t(X) %*% X, t(X) %*% y), qr(X)$rank and coef(lm(y ~ x)) report?

Hints

Hint 1 — the design matrix (B1, B2)

One row per sample, one column per unknown. The intercept \(a\) multiplies 1 in every row, so the first column is all 1’s; the slope \(b\) multiplies the dose, so the second column is \(\mathbf{x}\).

Hint 2 — residuals and orthogonality (B3, B5)

Fitted value \(=a + b\cdot\text{dose}\), residual \(=\) data \(-\) fitted. Then take dot products: \(\mathbf{1}\cdot\mathbf{r}\) is just the sum of the residuals, and \(\mathbf{x}\cdot\mathbf{r}\) multiplies each residual by its dose and adds. Least squares needs both to be zero.

Hint 3 — building the normal equations (B4, B8)

Each entry of \(X^\mathsf{T}X\) is a dot product of two columns of \(X\); each entry of \(X^\mathsf{T}\mathbf{y}\) is a dot product of a column with \(\mathbf{y}\). You get a square \(2\times2\) system. Subtract one equation from the other to eliminate \(a\).

Hint 4 — a single column (B6, B7)

With one column \(\mathbf{v}\), the plane is just a line and the fit is the Week 1 projection: coefficient \(= \dfrac{\mathbf{y}\cdot\mathbf{v}}{\mathbf{v}\cdot\mathbf{v}}\). For B7, think about which point a line without intercept is forced through.

Hint 5 — singular normal equations (B9)

If the two columns of \(X\) are equal, then \(a\,\mathbf{1} + b\,\mathbf{x} = (a + b)\,\mathbf{1}\): only the sum \(a + b\) matters. In R, qr(X)$rank smaller than ncol(X) flags dependent columns.

Solutions

Solution

B1. \(a + 0b = 1\), \(a + 1b = 3\), \(a + 2b = 2\). The first gives \(a = 1\), then the second gives \(b = 2\). The third would need \(1 + 2\cdot2 = 5 = 2\), which is false. No exact solution.

B2. \[ X = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 1 & 1 \\ 1 & 2 \end{pmatrix}, \qquad \mathbf{y} = \begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ 3 \\ 2 \end{pmatrix}. \]

B3. a. Fitted values \(1 + x\): \((1, 2, 3)\). Residual \(\mathbf{r} = (1, 3, 2) - (1, 2, 3) = (0, 1, -1)\). Sum of squares \(0 + 1 + 1 = 2\). b. \(\mathbf{1}\cdot\mathbf{r} = 0 + 1 - 1 = 0\), but \(\mathbf{x}\cdot\mathbf{r} = 0 + 1 - 2 = -1 \ne 0\). Not orthogonal to the dose column, so not the least-squares line: the residuals still have a trend with dose that a different slope could take up.

B4. \[ X^\mathsf{T}X = \begin{pmatrix} 3 & 0+1+2 \\ 0+1+2 & 0+1+4 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 3 & 3 \\ 3 & 5 \end{pmatrix}, \qquad X^\mathsf{T}\mathbf{y} = \begin{pmatrix} 1+3+2 \\ 0+3+4 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 6 \\ 7 \end{pmatrix}. \] So \(3a + 3b = 6\) and \(3a + 5b = 7\). Subtracting: \(2b = 1\), \(b = 0.5\); then \(3a = 6 - 1.5 = 4.5\), \(a = 1.5\). The least-squares line is \(y = 1.5 + 0.5x\).

B5. a. \(\hat{\mathbf{y}} = (1.5, 2, 2.5)\), \(\mathbf{r} = (1, 3, 2) - (1.5, 2, 2.5) = (-0.5, 1, -0.5)\). b. \(\mathbf{1}\cdot\mathbf{r} = -0.5 + 1 - 0.5 = 0\) and \(\mathbf{x}\cdot\mathbf{r} = 0 + 1 - 1 = 0\). Orthogonal to both columns. c. \(0.25 + 1 + 0.25 = 1.5\), smaller than the colleague’s \(2\).

B6. \(\dfrac{\mathbf{y}\cdot\mathbf{1}}{\mathbf{1}\cdot\mathbf{1}} = \dfrac{1 + 3 + 2}{3} = 2\), so \(\hat{\mathbf{y}} = (2, 2, 2)\). The fitted constant is the mean of \(\mathbf{y}\). The residual \((-1, 1, 0)\) sums to zero, i.e. it is orthogonal to \(\mathbf{1}\).

B7. \(\dfrac{\mathbf{y}\cdot\mathbf{x}}{\mathbf{x}\cdot\mathbf{x}} = \dfrac{0 + 3 + 4}{0 + 1 + 4} = \dfrac{7}{5} = 1.4\). Not the same as \(0.5\): without an intercept the line is forced through \((0, 0)\), so the slope has to do all the work of reaching the data, which sit around \(y = 2\). The two models project \(\mathbf{y}\) onto different spaces (a line versus a plane), so they give different answers.

B8. \[ A = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 \\ 1 & 1 \end{pmatrix}, \quad \mathbf{b} = \begin{pmatrix} 2 \\ 3 \\ 6 \end{pmatrix}, \quad A^\mathsf{T}A = \begin{pmatrix} 2 & 1 \\ 1 & 2 \end{pmatrix}, \quad A^\mathsf{T}\mathbf{b} = \begin{pmatrix} 8 \\ 9 \end{pmatrix}. \] So \(2x + y = 8\) and \(x + 2y = 9\). Twice the second minus the first: \(3y = 10\), \(y = 10/3 \approx 3.33\); then \(x = 9 - 20/3 = 7/3 \approx 2.33\). The same answer as qr.solve.

B9. a. \(X = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 1 & 1 \\ 1 & 1 \end{pmatrix}\), so \(X^\mathsf{T}X = \begin{pmatrix} 3 & 3 \\ 3 & 3 \end{pmatrix}\). The two rows are equal (the matrix is singular), so the normal equations \(3a + 3b = 6\), \(3a + 3b = 6\) are one equation in two unknowns: infinitely many solutions. b. Any pair with \(a + b = 2\), e.g. \((2, 0)\) and \((0, 2)\), or \((5, -3)\). All give fitted values \((2, 2, 2)\), the mean. The fit is fine, but the split between intercept and slope is not determined: with only one dose you cannot learn a dose effect.

R1. Yes: beta and coef(fit) are both \((1.5, 0.5)\), and t(X) %*% r prints zeros (on other data you may see tiny numbers like -8.9e-16: zero up to floating point).

R2. coef(lm(y ~ 1)) and mean(y) are both 2; coef(lm(y ~ x - 1)) and sum(x * y) / sum(x * x) are both 1.4.

R3.

A <- matrix(c(1, 0,
              0, 1,
              1, 1), nrow = 3, byrow = TRUE)
b <- c(2, 3, 6)
solve(t(A) %*% A, t(A) %*% b)   # 2.333333 3.333333
qr.solve(A, b)                  # 2.333333 3.333333

R4. With set.seed(1) both lines print the same estimates:

#   (Intercept)        dose
#     2.0449578   0.8335361

close to the true \(a = 2\), \(b = 0.8\) but not equal: the noise moves the estimates a little. t(X) %*% resid(...) is zero up to rounding, as always for a least-squares fit.

R5. solve stops with “Lapack routine dgesv: system is exactly singular”, qr(X)$rank is 1 (but ncol(X) is 2), and lm reports (Intercept) 2 and x NA: it keeps the first column and drops the redundant one.

Where this leads

  • Design matrices for covariates: the same \(\boldsymbol\beta\)-finding, but \(X\) gets more columns: treatment dummies, sex, age, batch. The fit is always the projection onto the column space of \(X\); the new work is building \(X\) and reading the coefficients. B9 is the simplest case of the dependent columns discussed there.
  • Design matrices in R: limma: limma fits exactly this least-squares model, gene by gene, with the design matrix you give it.
  • Penalized linear models: when \(X^\mathsf{T}X\) is singular or nearly so (more columns than samples, or almost-dependent columns), ridge regression adds a penalty that makes the normal equations solvable again.
  • Looking back at principal component analysis: PCA also projects, but the other way round: instead of projecting onto columns you chose, it finds the directions whose projection keeps the most of the data.
