Exercise: spectra as data

Goal

By the end of this exercise you should be able to:

  • explain why a spectrum is a vector and a spectral dataset is a samples × wavelengths matrix,
  • use the Beer–Lambert law \(A = \varepsilon\ell c\) to convert between absorbance and concentration by hand,
  • add pure-component spectra as a linear combination to build a mixture spectrum, and reason about recovering concentrations,
  • apply the four preprocessing ideas by hand on tiny examples — baseline correction, normalization, smoothing, first derivative — and say which artefact each removes,
  • explain why ordinary regression fails on spectra (\(p \gg n\), collinearity) and what PLS does instead.

This is an Applications unit: it reuses vectors and dot products, linear models, and principal component analysis. The by-hand parts are small on purpose; the R parts are illustrative (no spectroscopy packages are assumed installed).

Background

A spectrum is a vector. The instrument records absorbance \(A\) on a discrete grid of wavelengths \(\lambda_1,\dots,\lambda_p\), so one spectrum is

\[\mathbf{x} = (A(\lambda_1),\ \dots,\ A(\lambda_p)) \in \mathbb{R}^p,\]

and \(n\) samples stack as rows of an \(n\times p\) matrix \(X\) (samples × wavelengths).

Beer–Lambert law. For a single absorbing substance, \[A = \varepsilon\,\ell\,c,\] with \(c\) concentration, \(\ell\) path length, and \(\varepsilon\) the molar absorptivity at that wavelength. Absorbance is proportional to concentration.

Mixtures add. With \(k\) substances, the absorbances add at every wavelength, so the whole spectrum is a linear combination of pure-component spectra \(\mathbf{s}_i\) weighted by concentrations: \[\mathbf{x} = c_1\mathbf{s}_1 + \dots + c_k\mathbf{s}_k.\]

Preprocessing removes chemistry-free artefacts before modelling:

Step Removes One-line idea
Baseline correction additive offset/drift subtract a baseline
Normalization multiplicative scaling divide by length or SD
Smoothing random noise average neighbours (Savitzky–Golay)
Derivative baseline + peak overlap differentiate along \(\lambda\)

Calibration predicts concentration from a spectrum with a linear model \(\hat y = b_0 + \mathbf{b}\cdot\mathbf{x}\). Ordinary least squares fails because \(p \gg n\) and adjacent wavelengths are collinear; PLS compresses wavelengths into a few response-aware components instead.

By hand

B1. (Beer–Lambert, one substance). A purified compound has molar absorptivity \(\varepsilon = 2000\ \text{M}^{-1}\text{cm}^{-1}\) at its peak wavelength, measured in a cuvette of path length \(\ell = 1\ \text{cm}\). (a) A solution of concentration \(c = 3\times10^{-4}\ \text{M}\) is measured. What absorbance do you expect? (b) A different solution reads \(A = 0.9\). What is its concentration? (c) You halve the path length to \(0.5\ \text{cm}\) and re-measure the solution from (b). What absorbance now?

B2. (a calibration line). You measure absorbance at one wavelength for standards of known concentration:

\(c\) (mM) 0 1 2 4
\(A\) 0.00 0.20 0.40 0.80
  1. Do these obey Beer–Lambert? What is the slope \(\varepsilon\ell\) (in absorbance per mM)?
  2. An unknown reads \(A = 0.50\). Estimate its concentration.

B3. (a spectrum is a vector). Two samples are measured at \(p = 4\) wavelengths: \[\mathbf{x}_A = (0.1,\ 0.4,\ 0.2,\ 0.1), \qquad \mathbf{x}_B = (0.2,\ 0.8,\ 0.4,\ 0.2).\] (a) Compute the Euclidean distance \(\lVert \mathbf{x}_A - \mathbf{x}_B\rVert\). (b) Show that \(\mathbf{x}_B = 2\,\mathbf{x}_A\). What physical effect (from the lecture) could make one sample’s spectrum an exact multiple of another’s? (c) After vector normalization (divide each by its own length), would \(\mathbf{x}_A\) and \(\mathbf{x}_B\) be equal, or different? Explain without full computation.

B4. (mixtures add). Two pure components have spectra over \(p = 3\) wavelengths: \[\mathbf{s}_1 = (1,\ 0,\ 2), \qquad \mathbf{s}_2 = (0,\ 3,\ 1).\] (a) Write the mixture spectrum for concentrations \(c_1 = 2\), \(c_2 = 1\). (b) You observe a mixture spectrum \(\mathbf{x} = (3,\ 3,\ 7)\). Assuming it is \(c_1\mathbf{s}_1 + c_2\mathbf{s}_2\), find \(c_1\) and \(c_2\). (Hint: each wavelength gives one equation.) (c) In one sentence, why is knowing the pure-component spectra enough to turn spectroscopy into solving a linear system?

B5. (baseline correction). A spectrum over 5 wavelengths reads \[\mathbf{x} = (0.3,\ 0.7,\ 0.5,\ 0.9,\ 0.4).\] A second measurement of the same sample is shifted up by a constant baseline of \(0.2\): \[\mathbf{x}' = (0.5,\ 0.9,\ 0.7,\ 1.1,\ 0.6).\] (a) Apply constant-offset baseline correction by subtracting each spectrum’s own minimum. Write both corrected spectra. (b) Are the corrected spectra identical? Why is that the point of baseline correction?

B6. (a first derivative removes an offset). Take \(\mathbf{x} = (0.3,\ 0.7,\ 0.5,\ 0.9,\ 0.4)\) from B5. (a) Compute the first differences \(\Delta_i = x_{i+1} - x_i\) (four numbers). (b) Now do the same for the baseline-shifted \(\mathbf{x}' = \mathbf{x} + 0.2\). What do you notice, and why does this show a first derivative is blind to a constant baseline?

B7. (smoothing). Apply a moving average of window 3 to \(\mathbf{x} = (0.2,\ 0.8,\ 0.2,\ 0.8,\ 0.2)\), i.e. replace each interior point by the mean of itself and its two neighbours (leave the two ends out). (a) Write the three smoothed interior values. (b) The original alternates sharply; describe what smoothing did to that oscillation, and name the risk if the oscillation had been a real narrow peak.

B8. (why not ordinary regression). You have \(n = 8\) calibration samples, each a spectrum at \(p = 700\) wavelengths, and you want a linear model for concentration. (a) Why can ordinary least squares not give a unique coefficient vector here? (Think about \(p\) vs \(n\).) (b) Even if you had \(n = 1000\) samples, adjacent wavelengths are nearly identical columns of \(X\). What word describes this, and why does it still destabilise the coefficients? (c) In one sentence, what does PLS do differently that sidesteps both problems?

In R

The R below is illustrative — run it if you like, but the point is to connect the maths to code you can read. No spectroscopy packages are assumed.

A spectrum as a vector, a dataset as a matrix

# a tiny "dataset": 3 samples, 4 wavelengths
X <- matrix(c(0.1, 0.4, 0.2, 0.1,
              0.2, 0.8, 0.4, 0.2,
              0.15, 0.5, 0.25, 0.12),
            nrow = 3, byrow = TRUE)
dim(X)            # 3 4  -> 3 samples (rows), 4 wavelengths (cols)
X[1, ]            # the first spectrum, as a length-4 vector

# distance between two spectra (B3a)
sqrt(sum((X[1, ] - X[2, ])^2))

Beer–Lambert both ways (B1)

eps <- 2000; l <- 1
A_from_c <- function(c) eps * l * c
c_from_A <- function(A) A / (eps * l)

A_from_c(3e-4)    # expected absorbance
c_from_A(0.9)     # concentration from a reading

Build and unmix a mixture (B4)

s1 <- c(1, 0, 2)
s2 <- c(0, 3, 1)
mix <- 2 * s1 + 1 * s2      # c1 = 2, c2 = 1
mix                          # the mixture spectrum

# recover concentrations by solving the linear system S %*% c = x
S <- cbind(s1, s2)           # 3x2 pure-component matrix
x <- mix                     # observed mixture spectrum
# more equations (3) than unknowns (2): least-squares solve
solve(t(S) %*% S, t(S) %*% x)

Preprocessing on a toy spectrum

x  <- c(0.3, 0.7, 0.5, 0.9, 0.4)
xs <- x + 0.2                        # same sample, +0.2 baseline

# baseline correction: subtract each spectrum's own minimum (B5)
x  - min(x)
xs - min(xs)                          # identical to the line above

# first difference ~ first derivative (B6)
diff(x)
diff(xs)                              # identical: derivative kills the offset

# vector (L2) normalization (B3c)
x / sqrt(sum(x^2))

Hints

Hint 1 — Beer–Lambert both directions

\(A = \varepsilon\ell c\) rearranges to \(c = A/(\varepsilon\ell)\). Keep units consistent. Halving \(\ell\) halves \(A\) for the same \(c\), because \(A\) is proportional to \(\ell\) as well as \(c\).

Hint 2 — a calibration line

“Obeys Beer–Lambert” means \(A\) is a straight line through the origin in \(c\): \(A = (\varepsilon\ell)\,c\). Read the slope off any point, e.g. \(0.20/1 = 0.20\) absorbance per mM, and invert to get concentration from a new reading.

Hint 3 — spectra as vectors

Euclidean distance is \(\lVert\mathbf a-\mathbf b\rVert = \sqrt{\sum_i (a_i-b_i)^2}\) (the norm from Vectors and dot products). If \(\mathbf{x}_B = 2\mathbf{x}_A\) they point the same way but differ in length — think which multiplicative effect scales an entire spectrum. Vector normalization sets every spectrum’s length to 1, so two spectra on the same line become the same point.

Hint 4 — unmixing

A mixture spectrum is \(c_1\mathbf s_1 + c_2\mathbf s_2\). Writing it out wavelength by wavelength gives one linear equation per wavelength — a linear system in the unknowns \(c_1, c_2\). With more wavelengths than components the system is overdetermined; solve it in the least-squares sense (this is exactly what a linear model does). See solve.

Hint 5 — baseline correction

A constant baseline adds the same number to every entry. Subtracting the spectrum’s minimum cancels a constant offset, so two measurements that differ only by a constant collapse onto each other. This is the whole idea: same chemistry → same corrected spectrum.

Hint 6 — first derivative vs a constant baseline

The first difference \(x_{i+1}-x_i\) of a shifted spectrum \((x+b)\) is \((x_{i+1}+b)-(x_i+b) = x_{i+1}-x_i\) — the constant \(b\) cancels. That is the discrete version of “the derivative of a constant is zero.” See diff.

Hint 7 — moving average

Each interior smoothed value is the mean of three numbers: the point and its two neighbours, e.g. the second point becomes \((x_1+x_2+x_3)/3\). Averaging cancels an up-down-up oscillation. The risk: if that oscillation were a real narrow peak, smoothing would flatten it too — the smoothing/noise trade-off.

Hint 8 — why OLS fails

Ordinary least squares must invert \(X^\top X\), a \(p\times p\) matrix. With \(p > n\) it is singular (no unique solution — recall singular matrices from the linear-systems unit). With correlated columns (adjacent wavelengths) it is near-singular even when \(n>p\): this is collinearity, and it makes the coefficients huge and unstable. PLS avoids both by regressing on a few combinations of wavelengths chosen to correlate with the response.

Solutions

Solution

B1. (a) \(A = \varepsilon\ell c = 2000 \times 1 \times 3\times10^{-4} = 0.6\). (b) \(c = A/(\varepsilon\ell) = 0.9 / (2000\times1) = 4.5\times10^{-4}\ \text{M}\). (c) Absorbance is proportional to path length, so halving \(\ell\) halves \(A\): \(A = 0.9 \times 0.5 = 0.45\). (Concentration is unchanged; only the path shortened.)

B2. (a) Yes: \(A\) is exactly proportional to \(c\) (0, 0.20, 0.40, 0.80 for 0, 1, 2, 4 mM), a straight line through the origin. Slope \(\varepsilon\ell = 0.20/1 = 0.20\) absorbance per mM (every point gives the same ratio). (b) \(c = A/\text{slope} = 0.50 / 0.20 = 2.5\ \text{mM}\).

B3. (a) \(\mathbf{x}_A - \mathbf{x}_B = (0.1-0.2,\ 0.4-0.8,\ 0.2-0.4,\ 0.1-0.2) = (-0.1,-0.4,-0.2,-0.1)\). Distance \(=\sqrt{0.01+0.16+0.04+0.01} = \sqrt{0.22} \approx 0.469\). (b) Each entry of \(\mathbf{x}_B\) is twice the corresponding entry of \(\mathbf{x}_A\), so \(\mathbf{x}_B = 2\mathbf{x}_A\). A multiplicative effect — a longer path length (Beer–Lambert: \(A \propto \ell\)) or denser/thicker sample — scales the entire spectrum by a common factor. (c) They would be equal. Both lie on the same line through the origin (one is a scalar multiple of the other), so dividing each by its own length lands them on the same unit vector. Normalization removes exactly the multiplicative difference between them.

B4. (a) \(2\mathbf{s}_1 + 1\mathbf{s}_2 = 2(1,0,2) + (0,3,1) = (2,0,4) + (0,3,1) = (2,3,5)\). (b) Wavelength by wavelength, \(c_1\mathbf s_1 + c_2\mathbf s_2 = (c_1,\ 3c_2,\ 2c_1 + c_2)\). Matching to \((3,3,7)\): wavelength 1: \(c_1 = 3\); wavelength 2: \(3c_2 = 3 \Rightarrow c_2 = 1\); wavelength 3 checks: \(2(3)+1 = 7\) ✓. So \(c_1 = 3\), \(c_2 = 1\). (c) Because a mixture spectrum is a linear combination of the pure spectra, each wavelength contributes one linear equation in the unknown concentrations — so recovering concentrations is just solving a linear system.

B5. (a) \(\min(\mathbf{x}) = 0.3\), so \(\mathbf{x} - 0.3 = (0.0,\ 0.4,\ 0.2,\ 0.6,\ 0.1)\). For \(\mathbf{x}'\), \(\min = 0.5\), so \(\mathbf{x}' - 0.5 = (0.0,\ 0.4,\ 0.2,\ 0.6,\ 0.1)\). (b) They are identical. The two measurements differed only by a constant baseline, and subtracting each spectrum’s own minimum removes that constant — so the same chemistry yields the same corrected spectrum, which is exactly the goal of baseline correction.

B6. (a) \(\Delta = (0.7-0.3,\ 0.5-0.7,\ 0.9-0.5,\ 0.4-0.9) = (0.4,\ -0.2,\ 0.4,\ -0.5)\). (b) For \(\mathbf{x}' = \mathbf{x} + 0.2\), every difference is \((x_{i+1}+0.2)-(x_i+0.2) = x_{i+1}-x_i\) — the same four numbers \((0.4,-0.2,0.4,-0.5)\). The constant baseline cancels in the differencing, so a first derivative is blind to a constant offset (the discrete “derivative of a constant is zero”).

B7. (a) Interior means of window 3: point 2 \(\to (0.2+0.8+0.2)/3 = 0.4\); point 3 \(\to (0.8+0.2+0.8)/3 = 0.6\); point 4 \(\to (0.2+0.8+0.2)/3 = 0.4\). Smoothed interior \(= (0.4,\ 0.6,\ 0.4)\). (b) The sharp up-down-up oscillation was flattened toward its average — noise-like wiggles are suppressed. The risk: had that oscillation been a genuine narrow peak, the same averaging would have blunted or erased it. This is the smoothing/noise trade-off (a wider window smooths more but loses more real detail).

B8. (a) OLS requires inverting \(X^\top X\), a \(700\times700\) matrix, but with only \(n = 8\) samples \(X^\top X\) has rank at most 8 and is singular — there is no unique solution, and infinitely many coefficient vectors fit the 8 samples perfectly while predicting nonsense on new ones (overfitting). (b) Collinearity (multicollinearity): adjacent wavelengths are almost identical columns, so \(X^\top X\) is near-singular even when \(n > p\). Its near-zero eigenvalues blow up the inverse, making the coefficients enormous and wildly unstable to tiny changes in the data. (c) PLS builds a few latent components — combinations of wavelengths chosen to correlate with the response — and regresses on those, so it never inverts the ill-conditioned \(X^\top X\) and is untroubled by having many correlated wavelengths.

Where this leads

  • Chemometrics II: using spectra for classification (which class/species/grade is this sample?), honest model validation, and interpreting which wavelengths drive a prediction back to chemistry.
  • Principal component analysis: PLS is PCA’s response-aware cousin; the PCA unit builds the compression idea that both Principal Component Regression and PLS rely on.
  • Linear models: calibration \(\hat y = b_0 + \mathbf{b}\cdot\mathbf{x}\) is a linear model on a wide, collinear design — the reason regularised and latent-variable regressions exist.
