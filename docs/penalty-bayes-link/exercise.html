Exercise: penalties as priors

Provisional unit

This bridge unit links the penalized-regression and Bayesian blocks and may later move to a dedicated statistics / machine-learning course. It assumes you have already met ridge and lasso as optimisation and Bayes’ theorem (prior / likelihood / posterior).

Goal

By the end of this exercise you should be able to:

  • state, in one sentence, why a penalty is the same thing as a prior,
  • turn maximise posterior into minimise (negative log-likelihood + negative log-prior) and read off the fit term and the penalty term,
  • match ridge ⇔ Gaussian prior and lasso ⇔ Laplace prior, and derive each penalty from the prior’s density,
  • explain the identity \(\lambda_{\text{ridge}} = \sigma^2/\tau^2\) and what it says about prior precision,
  • explain, from the shape at \(\beta = 0\), why lasso produces exact zeros but ridge does not,
  • translate a biological belief (polygenic vs sparse) into the matching prior/penalty.

This is a concept day. Spend most of your time on the by-hand reasoning; the R section only illustrates the ideas (a full glmnet treatment lives in Ridge and lasso regression).

Background

The bridge in one move. The posterior peak (the MAP estimate) is

\[\hat{\boldsymbol\beta}_{\text{MAP}} = \arg\max_{\boldsymbol\beta}\ p(\boldsymbol\beta\mid\text{data}) = \arg\min_{\boldsymbol\beta}\ \big[\underbrace{-\log p(\text{data}\mid\boldsymbol\beta)}_{\text{fit term}} \;+\; \underbrace{-\log p(\boldsymbol\beta)}_{\text{penalty term}}\big].\]

(We may take \(-\log\) because the logarithm is increasing, so it does not move the location of the peak; it turns the product prior \(\times\) likelihood into a sum.)

The fit term. With Gaussian noise, \(y_i = \mathbf{x}_i^\top\boldsymbol\beta + \varepsilon_i\), \(\varepsilon_i\sim\mathcal N(0,\sigma^2)\), the negative log-likelihood is \(\tfrac{1}{2\sigma^2}\,\text{RSS} + \text{const}\). So the fit term is just the residual sum of squares.

The penalty term is the negative log of whatever prior you place on the coefficients:

Prior on each \(\beta_j\) density \(\propto\) \(-\log\) prior \(\propto\) Penalty Method
Gaussian \(\mathcal N(0,\tau^2)\) \(e^{-\beta_j^2/2\tau^2}\) \(\sum_j \beta_j^2\) \(\lVert\boldsymbol\beta\rVert_2^2\) ridge
Laplace (scale \(b\)) \(e^{-|\beta_j|/b}\) \(\sum_j |\beta_j|\) \(\lVert\boldsymbol\beta\rVert_1\) lasso

Clearing the \(\tfrac{1}{2\sigma^2}\) from the fit term gives \(\lambda_{\text{ridge}} = \sigma^2/\tau^2\) and \(\lambda_{\text{lasso}} = 2\sigma^2/b\). In words: a large penalty is a tight, confident prior that effects are near zero.

By hand

B1. In one sentence each, translate between the two languages: (a) what does the penalty term correspond to on the Bayesian side? (b) what does the fit term (RSS) correspond to? (c) what point summary of the posterior does penalised regression compute — the mean, the mode/peak, or the median?

B2. Starting from the Gaussian prior density \(p(\beta_j) \propto \exp\!\big(-\beta_j^2/2\tau^2\big)\), take \(-\log\) and sum over \(j\) to show the penalty term is \(\tfrac{1}{2\tau^2}\lVert\boldsymbol\beta\rVert_2^2\) (up to a constant). Which method does this give?

B3. Do the same for the Laplace prior \(p(\beta_j) \propto \exp\!\big(-|\beta_j|/b\big)\): take \(-\log\), sum over \(j\), and read off the penalty. Which method does this give?

B4. Combine B2 with the Gaussian-noise fit term and show that \[\arg\min_{\boldsymbol\beta}\ \frac{1}{2\sigma^2}\text{RSS} + \frac{1}{2\tau^2}\lVert\boldsymbol\beta\rVert_2^2 \;=\; \arg\min_{\boldsymbol\beta}\ \text{RSS} + \lambda\lVert\boldsymbol\beta\rVert_2^2\] and give \(\lambda\) in terms of \(\sigma^2\) and \(\tau^2\). (Hint: multiply the whole objective by a constant that does not change the minimiser.)

B5. (prior precision) Using \(\lambda_{\text{ridge}} = \sigma^2/\tau^2\): (a) Fix \(\sigma^2 = 1\). Compute \(\lambda\) for a tight prior \(\tau^2 = 0.01\) and for a loose prior \(\tau^2 = 100\). Which gives stronger shrinkage? (b) What happens to \(\lambda\) as \(\tau^2 \to \infty\) (a “flat”, no-information prior), and what familiar estimator do you recover?

B6. (why lasso zeros out) Sketch (or describe) the Gaussian and Laplace densities on the same axes, both centred at \(0\). Focus on the behaviour right at \(\beta = 0\). Which one has a sharp cusp there, and why does that cusp let the corresponding method set coefficients exactly to zero while the other only shrinks them?

B7. (biology → prior) For each biological situation, state whether a Gaussian (ridge) or Laplace (lasso) prior better encodes the belief, and say why in one line: (a) A complex, polygenic trait where hundreds of variants each nudge the phenotype a little. (b) A drug whose response is thought to be driven by two or three key genes, the other 19 997 being irrelevant. (c) You want to hand a wet-lab colleague a short, testable list of candidate genes. (d) Predictors are strongly correlated co-expressed genes and you would rather shrink them together than arbitrarily drop all but one. (Hint: think about which prior, or blend of priors, keeps correlated coefficients similar.)

Explore: prior, likelihood and posterior

The demo shows two coefficients \((\beta_1,\beta_2)\). Green shading is the prior (darker = more prior belief), grey ellipses are the likelihood (centred on the OLS = maximum-likelihood point, which you can drag), purple contours are the posterior, and the purple dot is the posterior peak, the MAP, which is the penalized estimate. The demo fixes \(\sigma^2 = 1\), so the Gaussian prior has \(\tau^2 = 1/\lambda\) and the Laplace prior has \(b = 2/\lambda\). The right panel shows the MAP coefficients against \(\lambda\).

E1. With the Gaussian prior, move \(\lambda\) from \(0.1\) to \(10\). What happens to \(\tau^2\), to the prior shading and to the MAP? Which estimator do you approach as \(\lambda \to 0\) (compare B5)?

E2. Switch to the Laplace prior at \(\lambda = 2\). Where is the MAP, and is any coefficient exactly zero? Now lower \(\lambda\) to \(1\): is it still zero? What does that say about how strong the prior must be before it sets a coefficient to zero?

E3. Still with the Laplace prior at \(\lambda = 2\), look at the purple posterior contours where they cross the \(\beta_1\) axis. Are they smooth there, as they are for the Gaussian prior? Connect what you see to B6.

Solutions

E1. \(\tau^2 = 1/\lambda\) falls from \(10\) to \(0.1\): the prior circles shrink tightly around \(0\) (a more confident prior that effects are small). The MAP moves from close to the OLS point, about \((1.81, 0.61)\) at \(\lambda = 0.1\), to about \((0.20, 0.12)\) at \(\lambda = 10\): shrunk, but never exactly zero. As \(\lambda \to 0\) the prior becomes flat and the MAP becomes the OLS / maximum-likelihood estimate, as in B5(b).

E2. At \(\lambda = 2\) the MAP is about \((1.24, 0)\): it sits on the \(\beta_1\) axis and \(\hat\beta_2\) is exactly \(0\). At \(\lambda = 1\) it moves off the axis to about \((1.64, 0.24)\). The Laplace prior sets a coefficient to zero only once it is strong enough compared with the evidence for that coefficient (here from \(\lambda \approx 1.7\) on); weak effects are zeroed first, strong ones need a stronger prior.

E3. No: the Laplace posterior contours have a crease (a kink) along the axis, because the prior’s cusp at \(\beta = 0\) is carried into the posterior. The peak can sit right in that crease, which is why the MAP lands on exactly \(\beta_2 = 0\). The Gaussian prior is smooth at \(0\), so its posterior contours are smooth ellipses and their peak is almost never exactly on an axis (B6).

In R

The code below only illustrates today’s concepts — the shapes of the two priors and the reading of a fit as a MAP estimate. (Running glmnet in earnest is the job of Ridge and lasso regression; here we keep it to a static illustration since packages are not run at render time.)

The two prior shapes

# Gaussian vs Laplace densities, both centred at 0, matched to unit variance.
b_grid <- seq(-4, 4, length.out = 400)

gauss   <- dnorm(b_grid, mean = 0, sd = 1)          # Gaussian  N(0, 1)
laplace <- (1 / (2 * (1/sqrt(2)))) *                # Laplace, scale b = 1/sqrt(2)
           exp(-abs(b_grid) / (1/sqrt(2)))          #   -> also variance 1

plot(b_grid, laplace, type = "l", lwd = 2,
     xlab = "beta", ylab = "prior density")
lines(b_grid, gauss, lwd = 2, lty = 2)
legend("topright", c("Laplace (lasso)", "Gaussian (ridge)"),
       lwd = 2, lty = c(1, 2))
# Note the sharp CUSP of the Laplace at beta = 0  -> exact zeros (variable selection)
# vs the rounded top of the Gaussian            -> shrink but never exactly zero

The penalties are the negative log-priors

# Up to constants, the penalty term IS the negative log-prior.
neg_log_gauss   <- function(b, tau2 = 1) b^2 / (2 * tau2)   # -> ridge:  sum(beta^2)
neg_log_laplace <- function(b, bscale = 1) abs(b) / bscale  # -> lasso:  sum(|beta|)

neg_log_gauss(c(-2, 0, 2))    # 2 0 2      quadratic in beta
neg_log_laplace(c(-2, 0, 2))  # 2 0 2      linear in |beta|
# same values here by coincidence of inputs; the SHAPE differs (quadratic vs V-shaped)

Reading a glmnet call as a prior (illustration only)

library(glmnet)

# alpha selects the PRIOR SHAPE; lambda sets the PRIOR STRENGTH (precision).
fit_ridge <- glmnet(X, y, alpha = 0)     # Gaussian prior
fit_lasso <- glmnet(X, y, alpha = 1)     # Laplace  prior
fit_enet  <- glmnet(X, y, alpha = 0.5)   # blend    -> elastic net

# Cross-validating lambda = letting the DATA choose how confident a prior to hold.
cvfit  <- cv.glmnet(X, y, alpha = 1)
lambda <- cvfit$lambda.min               # small lambda = weak prior, large = strong

Tasks

R1. Run the “two prior shapes” block. Zoom the plot near \(\beta = 0\) (e.g. restrict b_grid to seq(-0.5, 0.5, length.out = 400)). Describe in words how the two curves differ at the origin, and connect that to B6.

R2. Evaluate neg_log_gauss and neg_log_laplace on a grid such as seq(-3, 3, 0.5) and plot each against beta. Confirm one is a parabola (\(\beta^2\)) and the other a V (\(|\beta|\)). Which corresponds to ridge and which to lasso?

R3. With \(\sigma^2 = 1\), compute lambda <- 1 / tau2 for tau2 <- c(0.01, 0.1, 1, 10, 100). Tabulate \(\tau^2\) against \(\lambda\) and state, in one sentence, the relationship between prior confidence and penalty strength (this is B5 in code).

Hints

Hint 1 — the master equation

Everything follows from taking \(-\log\) of posterior ∝ likelihood × prior. The \(-\log\) turns the product into a sum, and turns maximise into minimise: \(-\log(\text{likelihood}) + (-\log(\text{prior}))\). The first piece is the fit term, the second is the penalty term. See the summary table on the dnorm reference for the Gaussian density form.

Hint 2 — negative log of a Gaussian (B2, B4)

For \(p(\beta_j)\propto e^{-\beta_j^2/2\tau^2}\), \(-\log p(\beta_j) = \dfrac{\beta_j^2}{2\tau^2} + \text{const}\). Summing over \(j\) gives \(\dfrac{1}{2\tau^2}\sum_j\beta_j^2 = \dfrac{1}{2\tau^2}\lVert\boldsymbol\beta\rVert_2^2\) — the ridge (\(\ell_2\)) penalty. To get a clean \(\lambda\), multiply the whole objective by \(2\sigma^2\): minimisers are unchanged by scaling by a positive constant.

Hint 3 — negative log of a Laplace (B3)

For \(p(\beta_j)\propto e^{-|\beta_j|/b}\), \(-\log p(\beta_j) = \dfrac{|\beta_j|}{b} + \text{const}\). The absolute value in the exponent becomes an absolute value in the penalty: \(\dfrac{1}{b}\sum_j|\beta_j| = \dfrac{1}{b}\lVert\boldsymbol\beta\rVert_1\) — the lasso (\(\ell_1\)) penalty.

Hint 4 — prior precision (B5)

\(\lambda = \sigma^2/\tau^2\), and precision is \(1/\tau^2\). A small \(\tau^2\) (tight prior, high precision) makes \(\lambda\) large → strong shrinkage. As \(\tau^2\to\infty\) the prior goes flat, \(\lambda\to 0\), and MAP collapses to maximum likelihood = ordinary least squares.

Hint 5 — the cusp (B6)

Compare the two densities at the origin. The Gaussian \(e^{-\beta^2}\) has a rounded top with zero slope at \(0\), so it never forces a coefficient to be exactly \(0\). The Laplace \(e^{-|\beta|}\) has a kink / cusp at \(0\) — that corner is what lets the lasso land on coefficients that are exactly zero, giving variable selection.

Hint 6 — biology to prior (B7)

Ask: do I believe many small effects, or a few large ones? Many small effects (polygenic) → dense, smooth shrinkage → Gaussian / ridge. A few drivers, most exactly zero → sparse → Laplace / lasso. If you need a short candidate list, you want the exact zeros of lasso. For correlated predictors you want a blend — the elastic net (mixed Gaussian + Laplace prior) shrinks correlated coefficients together instead of arbitrarily keeping one.

Hint 7 — the R illustration

Build the density grid with seq, the Gaussian with dnorm, and the Laplace by hand as \(\tfrac{1}{2b}e^{-|\beta|/b}\) using abs and exp. Plot with plot / lines. In glmnet, alpha mixes the two penalties (prior shapes) and lambda sets strength.

Solutions

Solution

B1. (a) The penalty term is the negative log-prior on the coefficients — a penalty is a prior belief that coefficients are small. (b) The fit term (RSS) is the negative log-likelihood under Gaussian noise (up to the \(\tfrac{1}{2\sigma^2}\) factor and a constant). (c) Penalised regression finds the mode / peak of the posterior — the MAP estimate — not the posterior mean or median.

B2. Take the negative log of the Gaussian density: \[-\log p(\beta_j) = -\log\!\Big(c\,e^{-\beta_j^2/2\tau^2}\Big) = \frac{\beta_j^2}{2\tau^2} - \log c.\] The \(-\log c\) is a constant (no \(\boldsymbol\beta\)), so drop it. Summing over \(j\): \[-\log p(\boldsymbol\beta) = \frac{1}{2\tau^2}\sum_{j}\beta_j^2 = \frac{1}{2\tau^2}\lVert\boldsymbol\beta\rVert_2^2 + \text{const}.\] This is the sum-of-squares (\(\ell_2\)) penalty → ridge.

B3. Same steps with the Laplace density: \[-\log p(\beta_j) = -\log\!\Big(c\,e^{-|\beta_j|/b}\Big) = \frac{|\beta_j|}{b} - \log c.\] Drop the constant and sum: \[-\log p(\boldsymbol\beta) = \frac{1}{b}\sum_j |\beta_j| = \frac{1}{b}\lVert\boldsymbol\beta\rVert_1 + \text{const}.\] This is the sum-of-absolute-values (\(\ell_1\)) penalty → lasso.

B4. Add the Gaussian-noise fit term \(\tfrac{1}{2\sigma^2}\text{RSS}\) to the B2 penalty: \[\arg\min_{\boldsymbol\beta}\ \frac{1}{2\sigma^2}\text{RSS} + \frac{1}{2\tau^2}\lVert\boldsymbol\beta\rVert_2^2.\] Multiplying the whole objective by the positive constant \(2\sigma^2\) does not change where the minimum is: \[= \arg\min_{\boldsymbol\beta}\ \text{RSS} + \frac{2\sigma^2}{2\tau^2}\lVert\boldsymbol\beta\rVert_2^2 = \arg\min_{\boldsymbol\beta}\ \text{RSS} + \frac{\sigma^2}{\tau^2}\lVert\boldsymbol\beta\rVert_2^2.\] So \(\lambda = \dfrac{\sigma^2}{\tau^2}\) — the ridge penalty strength is the noise variance over the prior variance.

B5. (a) With \(\sigma^2 = 1\): tight prior \(\tau^2 = 0.01 \Rightarrow \lambda = 1/0.01 = 100\); loose prior \(\tau^2 = 100 \Rightarrow \lambda = 1/100 = 0.01\). The tight prior (\(\tau^2 = 0.01\)) gives the far larger \(\lambda\) and hence much stronger shrinkage — as it should, since a tight prior insists the coefficients are near zero. (b) As \(\tau^2 \to \infty\), \(\lambda = \sigma^2/\tau^2 \to 0\). The prior becomes flat (no information), the penalty vanishes, and the MAP reduces to ordinary least squares (= maximum likelihood).

B6. Both densities are bell-ish and centred at \(0\), but they behave differently at the origin. The Gaussian \(e^{-\beta^2/2\tau^2}\) is smooth with zero slope at \(0\) — a rounded top — so shrinking a coefficient toward \(0\) is never quite “worth” driving it all the way; ridge shrinks but leaves every \(\beta_j \ne 0\). The Laplace \(e^{-|\beta|/b}\) has a sharp cusp (kink) at \(0\) because of the \(|\beta|\): the penalty has a corner there. That corner means the optimum frequently sits exactly at \(\beta_j = 0\), so the lasso performs variable selection (exact zeros), whereas ridge does not.

B7. (a) Gaussian / ridge — a polygenic trait is many small nonzero effects, which is exactly a dense Gaussian prior that shrinks all coefficients gently without zeroing them. (b) Laplace / lasso — “a few drivers, the rest exactly irrelevant” is a sparse belief; the Laplace prior’s cusp zeroes out the irrelevant genes. (c) Laplace / lasso — you want the exact zeros so that only a short set of genes has nonzero coefficients, i.e. a testable candidate list. (d) A blend (elastic net) — pure lasso tends to pick one gene from a correlated group and zero the rest arbitrarily; adding a Gaussian (ridge) component shrinks correlated coefficients together. The elastic net is the MAP under a mixed Gaussian + Laplace prior.

R1. Restricting the grid to \([-0.5, 0.5]\) shows the Laplace forming a sharp point (cusp) at \(\beta = 0\) while the Gaussian is rounded there. This is the visual form of B6: the cusp is what enables lasso’s exact zeros; the rounded top means ridge only shrinks.

b_grid  <- seq(-0.5, 0.5, length.out = 400)
gauss   <- dnorm(b_grid, 0, 1)
laplace <- (1 / (2 * (1/sqrt(2)))) * exp(-abs(b_grid) / (1/sqrt(2)))
plot(b_grid, laplace, type = "l", lwd = 2, xlab = "beta", ylab = "density")
lines(b_grid, gauss, lwd = 2, lty = 2)   # Laplace has the pointed top; Gaussian is rounded

R2. The Gaussian negative-log is a parabola (\(\propto\beta^2\)) → ridge; the Laplace negative-log is a V (\(\propto|\beta|\)) → lasso.

beta <- seq(-3, 3, 0.5)
plot(beta, beta^2,  type = "b", ylab = "-log prior (up to const)")  # parabola -> ridge
plot(beta, abs(beta), type = "b", ylab = "-log prior (up to const)") # V-shape  -> lasso

R3. \(\lambda\) and \(\tau^2\) move in opposite directions: the more confident (smaller-variance, higher-precision) the prior, the larger the penalty.

tau2   <- c(0.01, 0.1, 1, 10, 100)
lambda <- 1 / tau2          # sigma^2 = 1, so lambda = sigma^2 / tau2
data.frame(tau2, precision = 1/tau2, lambda)
#   tau2 precision lambda
#  0.01     100.00 100.00   <- tight, confident prior -> strong penalty
#  0.10      10.00  10.00
#  1.00       1.00   1.00
# 10.00       0.10   0.10
# 100.00      0.01   0.01   <- loose prior            -> weak penalty

In one sentence: penalty strength is prior precision — a tighter (more confident) prior that effects are small corresponds to a larger \(\lambda\) and stronger shrinkage.

Where this leads

  • Bayesian inference: here we found only the posterior peak (MAP). A full Bayesian treatment reports the whole posterior — the peak and its width — giving credible intervals on each coefficient that the MAP alone cannot.
  • Ridge and lasso regression: the penalties, the geometry of the \(\ell_1\)/\(\ell_2\) constraint, cross-validating \(\lambda\), and the elastic net in practice with glmnet.
  • Hierarchical / empirical-Bayes models: rather than fixing the prior variance \(\tau^2\) (or cross-validating \(\lambda\)), one can put a prior on it too and let the data estimate how strong the prior should be — the principled version of tuning \(\lambda\).
