Quiz: confounding & Simpson’s paradox

Short questions on adjustment, confounding, Simpson’s paradox, design and interactions. Try each on paper first, then open the answer. Most are in the style of the on-campus exam (no computer).

Q1. In one sentence, what is a confounder? Give one example from an RNA-seq experiment.

Hint

Think of the two arrows coming out of it.

Answer

A confounder is a variable that affects both the predictor of interest (e.g. who got the treatment) and the outcome, and is not itself caused by the predictor. Example: sequencing batch when most controls were sequenced in one batch and most treated samples in another, and the batches read at different levels (others: age, sex, BMI if they differ between groups).

Q2. Expression of one gene in 8 mice, drug vs control. Females have a baseline of 30, males of 10, and within each sex the drug adds 4:

F M
ctrl 3 mice, expr 30 1 mouse, expr 10
treat 1 mouse, expr 34 3 mice, expr 14
  1. Compute the unadjusted difference of group means, treat − ctrl.
  2. What would lm(expr ~ treatment + sex) give for treatmenttreat and for sexM?
  3. Is this Simpson’s paradox? Why?
Hint

Group mean = sum of the four values / 4. For (b), compare mice of the same sex.

Answer
  1. ctrl: \((30+30+30+10)/4 = 100/4 = 25\). treat: \((34+14+14+14)/4 = 76/4 = 19\). Difference \(= -6\).
  2. treatmenttreat \(= +4\) (\(34-30\) and \(14-10\)), sexM \(= -20\) (\(10 - 30\)).
  3. Yes: the unadjusted effect (\(-6\)) and the within-sex effect (\(+4\)) have opposite signs. Sex confounds the comparison, because the treated group is mostly male and males have much lower expression. Check: \(4 + (-20)\times(3/4 - 1/4) = 4 - 10 = -6\).

Q3. In an additive model the adjusted treatment effect is \(3\) and the batch-B effect is \(8\). Three quarters of the treated samples but only one quarter of the controls were sequenced in batch B. What is the unadjusted difference of group means?

Hint

Unadjusted \(=\) effect \(+\) (batch effect) × (share in B among treated − share in B among controls).

Answer

\(3 + 8\times(3/4 - 1/4) = 3 + 8\times\tfrac12 = 3 + 4 = 7\). The unadjusted comparison overstates the effect by 4, because the treated samples are over-represented in the higher-reading batch.

Q4. For each treatment × batch count table, say whether the design is balanced, partially confounded or perfectly confounded, and whether lm(expr ~ treatment + batch) can estimate the treatment effect.

A B
ctrl 3 3
treat 3 3
A B
ctrl 6 0
treat 0 6
A B
ctrl 5 1
treat 2 4
Hint

Look for empty cells. With every control in A and every treated sample in B, which two columns of \(X\) are identical?

Answer
  1. Balanced — the adjusted and unadjusted estimates agree; adjusting for batch only removes noise.
  2. Perfectly confounded — the treatmenttreat and batchB columns of \(X\) are identical, \(X\) is not of full column rank, and there are infinitely many solutions. lm prints NA for batchB; the treatment effect cannot be estimated. Only a new, balanced design fixes it.
  3. Partially confounded — every cell has samples, so the columns differ and \(\boldsymbol\beta\) is unique. The unadjusted estimate is biased, but lm(expr ~ treatment + batch) gives the within-batch treatment effect.

Q5. In the canonical Simpson example, lm(expr ~ treatment) gives treatmenttreat \(= -3\) and lm(expr ~ treatment + batch) gives \(+2\). A student says: “The first model is wrong.” Do you agree? Explain in two sentences.

Hint

What question does each coefficient answer?

Answer

Not quite: \(-3\) is the correct difference between the treated and control samples as they were collected, so the arithmetic is right. But it answers a different question — it mixes the drug effect with the accident that most controls were in the higher-reading batch — whereas \(+2\) answers “within the same batch, what does the drug do?”, which is what we mean by the drug effect.

Q6. For expr ~ treatment * sex (references ctrl, F) you fit \(\beta_0 = 8\), \(\beta_1 = -1\) (treatmenttreat), \(\beta_2 = 2\) (sexM), \(\beta_3 = 4\) (treatmenttreat:sexM). (a) What is the treatment effect in females, and in males? (b) What is the predicted mean for treated males?

Hint

\(\beta_1\) is the effect in the reference sex; add \(\beta_3\) for the other sex. A treated male has all three dummies equal to 1.

Answer
  1. Females: \(\beta_1 = -1\) (the drug slightly lowers expression). Males: \(\beta_1 + \beta_3 = -1 + 4 = 3\) (it raises it). The effect differs, even in sign, between the sexes — an interaction.
  2. \(\beta_0 + \beta_1 + \beta_2 + \beta_3 = 8 - 1 + 2 + 4 = 13\).

Q7. In the canonical Simpson example the drug effect is \(+2\) in batch A and \(+2\) in batch B. Is batch a confounder, an effect modifier (interaction), both, or neither?

Hint

Does adding batch change the estimate? Is the effect different between batches?

Answer

A confounder only. Adding batch changes the treatment estimate (\(-3 \to +2\)), so it confounds; but the treatment effect is the same in both batches, so there is no interaction (\(\beta_3\) for treatment:batch would be 0).

Q8. A study asks whether a drug changes the expression of a gene in tumours. Which of these would you not adjust for, and why? (a) patient age at diagnosis; (b) sequencing batch; (c) tumour size measured after 4 weeks of treatment; (d) both body weight and BMI.

Hint

Was it fixed before the drug? Could the drug have changed it? Do two of them carry the same information?

Answer

Adjust for (a) and (b). Do not adjust for (c): tumour size after treatment is changed by the drug (a mediator or consequence; if the gene also affects tumour growth, a collider), so adjusting removes part of the effect or creates bias. For (d), use one of the two: weight and BMI are strongly collinear, so with both in the model their individual coefficients become unstable even though the predictions are fine.

Q9. Your design is perfectly balanced (each batch has the same number of controls and treated samples). Is it then pointless to put batch in the model?

Hint

Balance removes bias. What else does a batch effect do to the residuals?

Answer

No. With balance, adding batch does not change the treatment estimate (the bias is zero), but a batch effect left in the residuals inflates the noise. Adjusting for batch removes that variation, so the treatment effect is estimated more precisely (smaller standard error) — at the cost of one column per extra batch level.
