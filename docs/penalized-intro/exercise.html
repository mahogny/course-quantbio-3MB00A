Exercise: why penalize?

Goal

By the end of this exercise you should be able to:

  • recognise the p ≫ n shape of omics data and say why it breaks ordinary least squares,
  • explain, using rank, why \(X^\top X\) has no inverse when there are more predictors than samples,
  • describe how correlated predictors make \(X^\top X\) nearly singular and coefficients unstable,
  • explain the bias–variance tradeoff in plain words and say which end OLS sits at,
  • do a one-parameter ridge calculation by hand and see the coefficient shrink as \(\lambda\) grows,
  • explain why adding \(\lambda I\) to \(X^\top X\) restores invertibility (eigenvalue shift).

This is a concept day. Most questions are reasoning, not arithmetic; the point is to internalise why penalization is needed before we meet the specific penalties (ridge and lasso) in later units.

Provisional block

This block is provisional and may move to a later statistics / machine-learning course. Nothing here is guaranteed exam material — do it to build intuition, not to memorise.

Background

For a linear model \(\mathbf{y} \approx X\boldsymbol{\beta}\) with an \(n \times p\) design matrix \(X\) (\(n\) samples, \(p\) predictors), ordinary least squares (OLS) minimises the squared error and solves the normal equations:

\[X^\top X\,\boldsymbol{\beta} = X^\top \mathbf{y} \qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad \hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}} = (X^\top X)^{-1} X^\top \mathbf{y}.\]

This only works if the \(p \times p\) matrix \(X^\top X\) is invertible. Two things go wrong in biology:

  1. \(p > n\) (more genes than samples). Then \(\text{rank}(X^\top X) \le n < p\), so \(X^\top X\) is singular — no inverse, no unique solution.
  2. Correlated predictors (co-regulated genes). Then \(X^\top X\) is nearly singular — it has an eigenvalue close to 0, the inverse blows up, and coefficients become huge and unstable.

Penalized regression adds a price for coefficient size:

\[\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}} = \arg\min_{\boldsymbol{\beta}} \ \|\mathbf{y} - X\boldsymbol{\beta}\|^2 + \lambda\,(\text{size of }\boldsymbol{\beta}),\qquad \lambda \ge 0.\]

Using the squared size \(\|\boldsymbol{\beta}\|^2\) (ridge) changes the normal equations to

\[\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}} = (X^\top X + \lambda I)^{-1} X^\top \mathbf{y}.\]

Adding \(\lambda I\) lifts every eigenvalue of \(X^\top X\) by \(\lambda\), so \(X^\top X + \lambda I\) is always invertible for \(\lambda > 0\). The price is bias: coefficients are shrunk toward zero. \(\lambda\) is the bias–variance dial.

By hand

B1. For each experiment, write the shape of \(X\) as \(n \times p\) and state whether \(X^\top X\) (which is \(p \times p\)) can possibly be inverted for an OLS fit. Give the one-line reason.

  1. 200 patients, 5 clinical variables.
  2. 30 patients, 20,000 genes.
  3. 50 cells, 50 genes, all measurements distinct and uncorrelated.

B2. You have \(n = 2\) samples and \(p = 3\) predictors, so \(\mathbf{y} \approx X\boldsymbol{\beta}\) is 2 equations in 3 unknowns. Without computing anything, explain how many \(\boldsymbol{\beta}\) vectors fit the data exactly, and connect this to “the model is unidentifiable.” (Recall the solution-space language from the Linear systems units.)

B3. Two genes are almost perfectly correlated, giving

\[X^\top X = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 1 & 1.01 \end{pmatrix}.\]

  1. Compare the two columns of \(X^\top X\). Is the matrix close to singular? (b) Find \((X^\top X)^{-1}\) by row-reducing \([\,X^\top X \mid I\,]\) (as in the warm-up on the \(2\times2\) inverse). How large are its entries? (c) What does that do to \(\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}} = (X^\top X)^{-1}X^\top\mathbf{y}\)?

B4. (one-parameter ridge — the key calculation). Take the simplest possible model: one predictor, no intercept, so \(y \approx \beta x\) over the samples. The ridge objective is

\[f(\beta) = \sum_i (y_i - \beta x_i)^2 + \lambda \beta^2.\]

  1. Differentiate with respect to \(\beta\), set to zero, and show \[\hat{\beta} = \frac{\sum_i x_i y_i}{\sum_i x_i^2 + \lambda}.\]
  2. With \(\sum x_i y_i = 10\) and \(\sum x_i^2 = 5\), compute \(\hat{\beta}\) for \(\lambda = 0,\ 5,\ 20\).
  3. What happens to \(\hat{\beta}\) as \(\lambda \to \infty\)? What is \(\hat{\beta}\) at \(\lambda = 0\), and what is that estimator called?

B5. (eigenvalue shift). Let \(X^\top X = \begin{pmatrix} 4 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 \end{pmatrix}\) (a rank-1 matrix — the second direction carries no information, as if \(p > n\)).

  1. What are its eigenvalues? Is it invertible?
  2. Form \(X^\top X + \lambda I\) with \(\lambda = 2\). What are its eigenvalues now?
  3. Is it invertible? Explain in one sentence why adding \(\lambda I\) always fixes the singular case.

B6. (bias–variance in words). For each description, say whether it is mainly a bias problem or a variance problem, and whether the model is under- or over-fitting:

  1. A model fits the 30 training patients perfectly but predicts new patients terribly.
  2. A model predicts a constant (the overall mean) for everyone, ignoring gene expression.
  3. Refitting the model after swapping 3 patients flips several coefficients from large positive to large negative.

In R

Use R to see the failures and the fix. These are static illustrations — the point is what the output would show, not to run them now.

OLS breaks when p > n

set.seed(1)
n <- 5; p <- 8                       # more predictors than samples
X <- matrix(rnorm(n * p), n, p)
XtX <- t(X) %*% X                    # 8 x 8, but built from only 5 samples

qr(XtX)$rank                         # 5, not 8  -> rank-deficient
solve(XtX)                           # ERROR: system is computationally singular

A near-singular X^T X blows up the inverse

XtX <- matrix(c(1, 1,
                1, 1.01), 2, byrow = TRUE)
solve(XtX)                           # entries around 100 -> huge, unstable

The ridge fix: add lambda to the diagonal

XtX <- matrix(c(4, 0,
                0, 0), 2, byrow = TRUE)   # singular (rank 1)
lambda <- 2
XtX_ridge <- XtX + lambda * diag(2)       # add lambda to the diagonal
XtX_ridge                                  # 6 0 / 0 2
solve(XtX_ridge)                           # now invertible: diag(1/6, 1/2)

One-parameter ridge shrinks the coefficient

sxy <- 10; sxx <- 5                  # sum(x*y) and sum(x^2)
for (lambda in c(0, 5, 20)) {
  beta <- sxy / (sxx + lambda)
  cat("lambda =", lambda, "  beta =", beta, "\n")
}
# lambda = 0  -> 2.0 (this is OLS)
# lambda = 5  -> 1.0
# lambda = 20 -> 0.4  (shrinking toward 0)

Tasks

R1. Using the ridge-shift block, change \(\lambda\) to \(0.001\) and to \(100\). Confirm that a tiny \(\lambda\) barely changes \(X^\top X\) (close to OLS) while a large \(\lambda\) dominates the diagonal (heavy shrinkage). Relate this to the bias–variance dial.

R2. For the one-parameter ridge loop, add \(\lambda = 100\) and \(\lambda = 1000\). Describe in one sentence the trajectory of \(\hat{\beta}\) as \(\lambda\) grows, and name the value it approaches.

R3. In the near-singular block, change the 1.01 to 1.0001. Predict what happens to the size of solve(XtX) before reading it off, then check your prediction.

Hints

Hint 1 — rank and invertibility (B1, B2)

\(X^\top X\) is \(p \times p\) but its rank cannot exceed the number of samples \(n\): \(\text{rank}(X^\top X) \le \min(n, p)\). A \(p \times p\) matrix is invertible only if its rank equals \(p\). So if \(p > n\), the rank is at most \(n < p\) and the matrix is singular. In R the rank is qr(XtX)$rank.

Hint 2 — infinitely many solutions (B2)

Fewer independent equations than unknowns means the solution set is not a single point but a whole space of vectors, all fitting equally well. This is the same “infinitely many solutions” case you met when solving \(A\mathbf{x}=\mathbf{b}\) with a rank-deficient \(A\) in the Linear systems units. “Unidentifiable” just means the data cannot single out one \(\boldsymbol{\beta}\).

Hint 3 — a nearly singular matrix and its inverse (B3)

Two columns that are almost parallel make a matrix nearly singular: row reduction leaves an almost-zero row on the left. Here R2 \(-\) R1 gives the row \((0,\ 0.01)\), and turning that \(0.01\) into a \(1\) means multiplying the row by \(100\) — which makes the inverse’s entries huge. Those large numbers multiply through to \(\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}\), giving huge, unstable coefficients. Check with solve. (Bonus, not for exam: the determinant \(ad - bc\) is the number that sits in the denominator of the inverse; here it is \(0.01\).)

Hint 4 — differentiating the ridge objective (B4)

\(f(\beta) = \sum_i (y_i - \beta x_i)^2 + \lambda\beta^2\). Use the chain rule term by term: \(\frac{d}{d\beta}(y_i-\beta x_i)^2 = 2(y_i-\beta x_i)(-x_i)\), and \(\frac{d}{d\beta}(\lambda\beta^2)=2\lambda\beta\). Set the total to zero, divide by 2, and collect the \(\beta\) terms: \(\beta(\sum x_i^2 + \lambda) = \sum x_i y_i\). Notice that at \(\lambda = 0\) this is exactly the OLS slope \(\sum x_i y_i / \sum x_i^2\).

Hint 5 — eigenvalue shift (B5)

A diagonal matrix wears its eigenvalues on the diagonal. Adding \(\lambda I\) adds \(\lambda\) to every diagonal entry, hence lifts every eigenvalue by \(\lambda\) (this is the fact you proved with the identity shift in the eigenvalue unit). A matrix is invertible exactly when none of its eigenvalues is zero, so lifting a zero eigenvalue to \(\lambda > 0\) makes an otherwise-singular matrix invertible.

Hint 6 — bias vs variance (B6)

Bias = error from a model too rigid to capture the real pattern (predicts the same simple thing regardless of data → underfitting). Variance = error from a model so flexible it chases noise and changes wildly between samples (fits training data perfectly but generalises badly, coefficients lurch when data changes → overfitting).

Solutions

Solution

B1. (a) \(X\) is \(200 \times 5\), so \(n = 200 > p = 5\). \(X^\top X\) is \(5\times5\) and can reach full rank 5 — invertible (assuming the variables are not exactly redundant). OLS is fine. (b) \(X\) is \(30 \times 20000\), so \(p \gg n\). \(\text{rank}(X^\top X) \le 30 \ll 20000\), so the \(20000\times20000\) matrix is singular — not invertible. OLS has no unique solution. (c) \(X\) is \(50\times50\), so \(n = p\). If all measurements are genuinely distinct and uncorrelated, \(X\) has full rank 50 and \(X^\top X\) is invertible — but this is the knife-edge case, and any correlation among genes would push it toward singularity.

B2. Two equations in three unknowns: there are more free parameters (3) than constraints (2), so the exactly-fitting \(\boldsymbol{\beta}\) form a whole line (a one-dimensional solution space) — infinitely many vectors fit perfectly. The data cannot pick one out, so the model is unidentifiable. This is the same rank-deficient “infinitely many solutions” case from the Linear systems units, and it is exactly what \(p > n\) produces in general.

B3. (a) The columns \((1, 1)\) and \((1, 1.01)\) are almost identical — almost parallel — mirroring two almost-perfectly-correlated genes. With exactly parallel columns the matrix would be singular (no inverse); this one is nearly singular. (b) Row reduction: \[ \left[\begin{array}{cc|cc} 1 & 1 & 1 & 0 \\ 1 & 1.01 & 0 & 1 \end{array}\right] \xrightarrow{\;\text{R2}\,-\,\text{R1}\;} \left[\begin{array}{cc|cc} 1 & 1 & 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 0.01 & -1 & 1 \end{array}\right] \xrightarrow{\;100\,\text{R2}\;} \left[\begin{array}{cc|cc} 1 & 1 & 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 & -100 & 100 \end{array}\right] \xrightarrow{\;\text{R1}\,-\,\text{R2}\;} \left[\begin{array}{cc|cc} 1 & 0 & 101 & -100 \\ 0 & 1 & -100 & 100 \end{array}\right], \] so \((X^\top X)^{-1} = \begin{pmatrix}101 & -100\\ -100 & 100\end{pmatrix}\): entries around 100 — huge. The almost-zero row \((0,\ 0.01)\) had to be multiplied by 100. (c) Those large numbers multiply \(X^\top\mathbf{y}\), so \(\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}\) becomes enormous and unstable: a tiny change in the data (or the 1.01) swings the coefficients wildly. This is multicollinearity. (Bonus, not for exam: \(\det(X^\top X) = 1.01 - 1 = 0.01\), and the inverse is \(\frac{1}{0.01}\begin{pmatrix}1.01 & -1\\ -1 & 1\end{pmatrix}\).)

B4. (a) Differentiate: \[f'(\beta) = \sum_i 2(y_i - \beta x_i)(-x_i) + 2\lambda\beta = -2\sum_i x_i y_i + 2\beta\sum_i x_i^2 + 2\lambda\beta.\] Set \(f'(\beta) = 0\) and divide by 2: \[-\sum_i x_i y_i + \beta\Big(\sum_i x_i^2 + \lambda\Big) = 0 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \hat{\beta} = \frac{\sum_i x_i y_i}{\sum_i x_i^2 + \lambda}.\] (b) With \(\sum x_i y_i = 10\), \(\sum x_i^2 = 5\):

\(\lambda\) \(\hat{\beta} = 10/(5+\lambda)\)
0 \(10/5 = 2.0\)
5 \(10/10 = 1.0\)
20 \(10/25 = 0.4\)
  1. As \(\lambda \to \infty\) the denominator grows without bound, so \(\hat{\beta} \to 0\) — heavy penalty shrinks the coefficient to nothing. At \(\lambda = 0\), \(\hat{\beta} = \sum x_i y_i / \sum x_i^2 = 2.0\), which is the ordinary least squares slope. So ridge is a family of estimators indexed by \(\lambda\), with OLS at one end and zero at the other.

B5. (a) \(X^\top X = \begin{pmatrix}4&0\\0&0\end{pmatrix}\) is diagonal, so its eigenvalues are the diagonal entries 4 and 0. Because one eigenvalue is 0, the matrix is singular — not invertible (the second direction carries no information, like a \(p>n\) dead direction). (b) \(X^\top X + 2I = \begin{pmatrix}6&0\\0&2\end{pmatrix}\), with eigenvalues 6 and 2 — every eigenvalue lifted by \(\lambda = 2\). (c) Now no eigenvalue is zero, so the matrix is invertible. Adding \(\lambda I\) raises every eigenvalue by \(\lambda\); any zero eigenvalue becomes \(\lambda > 0\), so the singular case is always cured. This is exactly why the ridge normal equations \(X^\top X + \lambda I\) are solvable even when \(p > n\).

B6. (a) Variance problem — the model is flexible enough to fit the training data perfectly but the fit does not generalise. Overfitting. (b) Bias problem — the model is too rigid (predicts a constant) to capture any real signal. Underfitting. (c) Variance problem — coefficients swinging violently when the data changes slightly is the signature of high variance (here driven by multicollinearity). Overfitting / instability. Shrinkage would calm this.

R1. With \(\lambda = 0.001\), \(X^\top X + \lambda I \approx \begin{pmatrix}4.001&0\\0&0.001\end{pmatrix}\) — almost unchanged from the singular original, so we are essentially back at OLS (low bias, high variance; the tiny second eigenvalue still makes the inverse large). With \(\lambda = 100\), the matrix is \(\begin{pmatrix}104&0\\0&100\end{pmatrix}\) — dominated by the penalty, so coefficients are heavily shrunk (high bias, low variance). Sliding \(\lambda\) from small to large moves you across the bias–variance dial.

R2. Extending the loop:

\(\lambda\) \(\hat{\beta}=10/(5+\lambda)\)
0 2.0
5 1.0
20 0.4
100 ≈ 0.095
1000 ≈ 0.00995

\(\hat{\beta}\) decreases monotonically toward 0 as \(\lambda\) grows — the coefficient is shrunk ever harder and approaches zero in the limit.

R3. With 1.0001, the columns are even closer to parallel: row reduction now leaves the row \((0,\ 0.0001)\), which must be multiplied by 10,000. So solve(XtX) has entries around 10,000 (exactly \(\begin{pmatrix}10001 & -10000\\ -10000 & 10000\end{pmatrix}\)), 100 times larger than before. Prediction: closer to singular, larger and more unstable inverse — confirmed by running it. (Bonus, not for exam: the determinant drops from \(0.01\) to \(0.0001\).) This is why highly correlated genes are so damaging without a penalty.

Where this leads

  • Ridge and lasso (this block): the two standard penalties. Ridge uses \(\|\boldsymbol{\beta}\|^2\) (the calculation you did in B4, generalised); lasso uses \(\sum|\beta_j|\) and drives coefficients exactly to zero, performing gene selection.
  • Choosing \(\lambda\) (this block): cross-validation picks the penalty strength that minimises prediction error on held-out data, rather than guessing.
  • PCA / dimensional reduction (Applications block): another route through the \(p \gg n\) problem — reduce the number of predictors instead of penalizing them.
