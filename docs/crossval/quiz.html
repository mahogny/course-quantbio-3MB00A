Quiz: cross-validation & model selection

Short questions to test your understanding of cross-validation and honest model selection. Try each on paper first, then open the answer. Several are in the style of the on-campus exam (no computer).

Q1. In one sentence, why is the error measured on your training data a poor estimate of how the model will do on new patients?

Hint

What can a flexible model do to the data it was fitted on, especially when there are far more genes than samples?

Answer

A flexible model can fit the noise of the training samples, so its training error is optimistically low; the honest test is data the model never saw during fitting. The bias is worst when \(p \gg n\) (many genes, few patients).

Q2. You run 10-fold CV on 90 samples. How large is each fold, how many samples does each model train on, and how many models are fitted?

Hint

Fold size is \(n/k\); each model trains on the other \(k-1\) folds; one model per fold.

Answer

Fold size \(= 90/10 = 9\) samples. Each model trains on the other \(9\) folds \(= 90 - 9 = 81\) samples. 10 models are fitted (one per held-out fold), and every sample is validated exactly once.

Q3. What are the two distinct jobs cross-validation does in a penalized-regression workflow?

Hint

One job is about choosing a knob; the other is about reporting a number.

Answer
  1. Tuning — choosing the penalty strength \(\lambda\) (the value that minimises CV error), and (2) estimation — giving an honest estimate of predictive performance. If the same CV both chose \(\lambda\) and reports the number, the number is mildly optimistic, which is why a separate held-out test set (or nested CV) is needed for the final estimate.

Q4. In cv.glmnet, what is the difference between lambda.min and lambda.1se, and why might you prefer lambda.1se?

Hint

One is the outright minimum; the other applies a “within one standard error” rule and gives a simpler model.

Answer

lambda.min is the \(\lambda\) with the lowest CV error. lambda.1se is the largest \(\lambda\) (most shrinkage, fewest genes) whose CV error is still within one standard error of that minimum. Since the two are statistically indistinguishable, lambda.1se gives a simpler, more reproducible signature — usually the better scientific choice for a biomarker list.

Q5. A student filters 20,000 genes down to the 200 most correlated with disease using all samples, then cross-validates a lasso on those 200 genes and reports 95% accuracy. What is wrong, and how do you fix it?

Hint

Did the gene filter look at the outcome of the samples that later become “held out”?

Answer

This is information leakage: the gene filter used the outcome \(y\) of every sample, including those later held out, so the folds are no longer naive and 95% is optimistically biased. Fix: move the gene selection inside each CV fold and compute it on the training rows only (or hold out a test set that no filtering step ever touches). Any step that uses \(y\) is part of fitting.

Q6. True or false: the test set may be used to decide which of several models to keep. Explain.

Hint

How many times may a genuine test set be used, and for what?

Answer

False. The moment you use the test set to choose something, it has influenced the model and is no longer an unbiased test — it has become a validation set. A genuine test set is locked away and opened once, only to report the final performance. Model selection belongs to the validation (CV) stage.

Q7. You are classifying a disease where only 6% of samples are cases. Why is plain accuracy a bad CV metric here, and what is a better one?

Hint

What accuracy does a model that always predicts “healthy” achieve?

Answer

A trivial model that always predicts “healthy” is 94% accurate, so accuracy rewards ignoring the cases entirely. AUC is better: it scores whether the model ranks cases above controls, is threshold-free, and is robust to class imbalance. (Deviance / log-loss is another honest choice.)

Q8. Training error is \(0.03\) and 10-fold CV error is \(0.28\). What does the gap tell you, and which way should you move \(\lambda\)?

Hint

A large gap between memorising the training data and doing well in CV is the fingerprint of one specific problem.

Answer

The large train–CV gap is the signature of overfitting: the model nearly memorises the training data but generalises poorly. Increase \(\lambda\) (shrink harder) to reduce flexibility and close the gap; trust the CV error (\(0.28\)), not the training error, as the estimate of real performance.

Q9. You have single-cell data with thousands of cells from each of 12 patients, and you want to predict a patient-level phenotype. Why is a plain random split of cells into folds misleading, and what should you do instead?

Hint

Are cells from the same patient independent? What happens if the same patient appears in both training and validation?

Answer

Cells from one patient are not independent, so a random cell-level split puts the same patient in both training and validation — the model can recognise the patient rather than the phenotype, leaking information and inflating performance. Use grouped CV: keep all cells from a patient together in one fold, so folds split at the patient level.
