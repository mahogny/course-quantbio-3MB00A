Exercise: principal component analysis

Goal

By the end of this exercise you should be able to:

  • explain, in words, what PCA does to a high-dimensional data cloud (rotate the axes to line up with the directions of greatest variance),
  • centre a small data matrix by hand and say why centring is necessary,
  • build the \(2\times2\) covariance matrix of a tiny dataset,
  • read its eigenvectors as principal components and its eigenvalues as variances (reusing the by-hand recipe from Eigenvalues & eigenvectors),
  • compute a score as the projection of a sample onto a PC, and read loadings off the eigenvector,
  • turn eigenvalues into variance explained.

This is a concept day. No prcomp yet — that arrives in PCA in R. Do the pen-and-paper parts first; the R section only confirms your hand answers.

Background

PCA takes a data matrix (samples × variables) and finds a new set of orthogonal axes — the principal components (PCs) — ordered so that the first captures the most variance, the second the most of what remains, and so on.

The pipeline (memorise this):

  1. Centre each column (subtract its mean). Optionally scale (divide by its standard deviation) if variables are on different units.
  2. Form the covariance matrix \(C\) (symmetric, \(p\times p\)): \(C_{ii} = \text{var}(\text{var}_i)\) on the diagonal, \(C_{ij} = \text{cov}(\text{var}_i,\text{var}_j)\) off it. For \(n\) samples use the sample covariance with divisor \(n-1\).
  3. Eigen-decompose \(C\). The eigenvectors are the PCs (orthogonal, because \(C\) is symmetric); the eigenvalues are the variances captured along each PC.
  4. Scores = projections of centred samples onto a PC: \(\text{score} = \mathbf{x}\cdot\mathbf{u}\) for centred sample \(\mathbf{x}\) and unit PC \(\mathbf{u}\). Scores describe samples.
  5. Loadings = the entries of the eigenvector — the weight of each variable in that PC. Loadings describe variables (genes).
  6. Variance explained by PC\(_k\) = \(\lambda_k / \sum_j \lambda_j\). The denominator equals the total variance (the trace of \(C\)).

Throughout we use a tiny dataset of 2 genes measured in 5 samples:

sample GENE_A GENE_B
s1 1 5
s2 3 3
s3 4 6
s4 5 7
s5 7 9

By hand

B1. Compute the mean of each gene, then write out the centred data matrix (subtract each column’s mean). Check that each centred column sums to 0. Why do we centre before PCA — what goes wrong if we skip it?

B2. Using the centred data, compute \(\text{var}(A)\), \(\text{var}(B)\), and \(\text{cov}(A,B)\) with divisor \(n-1 = 4\). Assemble the covariance matrix \(C\). Confirm it is symmetric.

B3. Find the eigenvalues of \(C\) (it has the symmetric pattern from Eigenvalues & eigenvectors). Verify by multiplying, and check them with the trace.

B4. Find a unit eigenvector for each eigenvalue. These are PC1 (largest eigenvalue) and PC2. Confirm they are orthogonal. Which one points along the long axis of the data cloud?

B5. (scores) Project each centred sample onto PC1 (the unit eigenvector \(\mathbf{u}_1\)) to get its PC1 score, using \(\text{score} = \mathbf{x}\cdot\mathbf{u}_1\). Which sample sits at each extreme of PC1?

B6. (loadings) Write down the loadings of PC1 (the entries of \(\mathbf{u}_1\)). Do the two genes contribute equally to PC1? What does that say biologically about how genes A and B behave?

B7. (variance explained) What fraction of the total variance does PC1 capture? PC2? Confirm the two fractions add to 1 and that the denominator equals the trace of \(C\).

B8. (concept) Suppose you had not centred, and instead ran the whole procedure on the raw numbers. In one or two sentences, explain why the leading direction would then mostly point from the origin toward the data’s centre, rather than along the direction of greatest spread.

Explore

Do the by-hand part first; then use this demo to see the same numbers move. The green axis can be turned by dragging a white handle or with the slider; the dashed lines are the perpendicular projections, the orange dots the scores, and the percentage is the variance of the scores out of the total variance. The five samples are the ones above, already centred.

E1. Set the axis angle to \(0^\circ\) (along GENE_A) and then to \(90^\circ\) (along GENE_B). What percentage is captured each time? Which entries of \(C\) from B2 are these numbers?

E2. Turn the axis to make the percentage as large as possible. At what angle, and how much variance is captured? Then find the angle that makes it as small as possible. Compare with your eigenvalues (B3) and variance explained (B7), then tick “Show PC1 and PC2”.

E3. At \(45^\circ\), compare the scores in the readout with your PC1 scores from B5. Then turn the axis to any other angle: what do “along the axis” and “perpendicular” always add up to, and why?

E4. Press Reset, then drag s3 from the centre to roughly \((-2, 2)\) (up and to the left, across the long axis). Does PC1 turn? What happens to the percentage of variance PC1 explains?

Solutions

E1. Both give 50%: at \(0^\circ\) the scores are just the centred GENE_A values, so the variance captured is \(\text{var}(A) = C_{11} = 5\) out of \(10\); at \(90^\circ\) it is \(\text{var}(B) = C_{22} = 5\) out of \(10\).

E2. The maximum is at \(45^\circ\): variance \(9\), i.e. 90% — this is \(\lambda_1\) and the PC1 direction \(\tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}(1,1)\). The minimum is at \(135^\circ\): variance \(1\), 10% — this is \(\lambda_2\) along PC2, perpendicular to PC1. No other angle beats 90%, which is what “PC1 is the direction of greatest variance” means.

E3. At \(45^\circ\) the scores are \(-2.83, -2.83, 0, 1.41, 4.24\), the same as B5. “Along” plus “perpendicular” is always 10, the total variance (the trace of \(C\)). For each centred sample, its squared distance from the centre splits into (score along the axis)\(^2\) + (distance from the axis)\(^2\) (Pythagoras), so turning the axis only moves variance between the two directions.

E4. PC1 stays at \(45^\circ\): s3 was moved exactly along the PC2 direction, so the spread along PC1 is still \(9\). But the spread across the long axis grows (PC2 goes from \(1\) to about \(2.6\)), so the total rises to about \(11.6\) and PC1 explains only about \(9/11.6 \approx 78\%\). A sample off the main trend adds variance PCA cannot summarise with PC1 alone. (If you drop it somewhere else, PC1 will also turn a little.)

In R

Use R only to confirm the hand answers. (In PCA in R we use prcomp; today we build each piece with base R so the mapping to the maths is explicit — no PCA function is called here.)

Centre the data and form the covariance matrix

X <- matrix(c(1, 5,
              3, 3,
              4, 6,
              5, 7,
              7, 9), ncol = 2, byrow = TRUE)
colnames(X) <- c("GENE_A", "GENE_B")

colMeans(X)              # 4 6  -> the two gene means

Xc <- scale(X, center = TRUE, scale = FALSE)   # centre only, no scaling
colMeans(Xc)             # ~0 0 -> columns now centred

C <- cov(Xc)             # 2x2 covariance matrix (divisor n-1 = 4)
C                        # [[5, 4], [4, 5]]

Eigen-decompose the covariance matrix (this is PCA, by hand)

E <- eigen(C)
E$values                 # 9 1   -> variances along PC1, PC2
E$vectors                # columns are the unit eigenvectors (PC1, PC2)

Scores: project centred samples onto the PCs

scores <- Xc %*% E$vectors   # each row: (PC1 score, PC2 score) for that sample
scores

Variance explained

E$values / sum(E$values)     # 0.9 0.1  -> PC1 90%, PC2 10%
sum(E$values)                # 10 = trace(C) = total variance
sum(diag(C))                 # 10  -> same total, from the trace directly

Tasks

R1. Reproduce your B2 covariance matrix with cov(Xc) and confirm it equals your hand answer.

R2. Run eigen(C) and match $values to your B3 eigenvalues and $vectors to your B4 eigenvectors. (Signs may be flipped — an eigenvector and its negative describe the same axis.)

R3. Compute scores <- Xc %*% E$vectors and check the first column against the B5 PC1 scores you found by hand.

R4. Confirm E$values / sum(E$values) gives the B7 variance-explained fractions, and that sum(E$values) equals sum(diag(C)).

Hints

Hint 1 — centring

Subtract each column’s own mean from every entry in that column. The mean of GENE_A is \((1+3+4+5+7)/5\) and of GENE_B is \((5+3+6+7+9)/5\). After centring, each column must sum to exactly 0 — a quick self-check. In R, scale(X, center = TRUE, scale = FALSE) centres without scaling.

Hint 2 — sample covariance

With centred values, \(\text{cov}(A,B) = \frac{1}{n-1}\sum_i (A_i-\bar A)(B_i-\bar B)\) and \(\text{var}(A)=\text{cov}(A,A)\). Here \(n=5\) so the divisor is \(4\). This matches R’s cov and var, so your hand answer should equal cov(Xc).

Hint 3 — eigenvalues of a 2×2

\(C=\begin{pmatrix}5&4\\4&5\end{pmatrix}\) has the form \(\begin{pmatrix}a&b\\b&a\end{pmatrix}\), so try \((1,1)\) and \((1,-1)\): the eigenvalues are \(a+b\) and \(a-b\). Check: \(\lambda_1+\lambda_2\) should equal the trace \(5+5\). (Bonus, not for exam: the characteristic equation \((5-\lambda)^2-16=0\) gives the same.)

Hint 4 — eigenvectors and normalising

Plug each \(\lambda\) into \((C-\lambda I)\mathbf v=\mathbf 0\) and read \(v_2\) in terms of \(v_1\) from one row. To make it a unit vector, divide by its length \(\sqrt{v_1^2+v_2^2}\); e.g. \((1,1)\) has length \(\sqrt2\), so the unit version is \(\frac{1}{\sqrt2}(1,1)\approx(0.707,0.707)\).

Hint 5 — scores are projections

A score is a dot product (the projection you met in vector geometry). For centred sample \(\mathbf x=(x_A,x_B)\) and unit PC \(\mathbf u=(u_A,u_B)\), the score is \(x_A u_A + x_B u_B\). In R, Xc %*% E$vectors does all samples and both PCs at once — column 1 is the PC1 scores.

Hint 6 — loadings vs scores

Loadings are the eigenvector entries (one weight per gene) — they describe how genes combine to build the PC. Scores are per-sample projections — where samples land. Same eigenvector, two readings: down the vector (loadings, genes) vs dotting a sample against it (scores, samples).

Hint 7 — variance explained

Divide each eigenvalue by the sum of all eigenvalues: \(\lambda_k/\sum_j\lambda_j\). The sum of eigenvalues equals the trace of \(C\) (sum of the diagonal, sum(diag(C))), which is the total variance — so the fractions are guaranteed to add to 1.

Solutions

Solution

B1. Means: \(\bar A = (1+3+4+5+7)/5 = 20/5 = 4\) and \(\bar B = (5+3+6+7+9)/5 = 30/5 = 6\). Centred data (subtract the column mean):

sample \(A-\bar A\) \(B-\bar B\)
s1 \(-3\) \(-1\)
s2 \(-1\) \(-3\)
s3 \(0\) \(0\)
s4 \(1\) \(1\)
s5 \(3\) \(3\)

Column sums: \(-3-1+0+1+3 = 0\) and \(-1-3+0+1+3 = 0\) ✓. We centre because PCA describes variation around the mean. If we skip it, the leading direction is pulled toward the data’s centroid (the mean vector) instead of lying along the direction of greatest spread — so PC1 would partly encode where the cloud sits, not how it is shaped (see B8).

B2. With divisor \(n-1 = 4\): \[\text{var}(A) = \tfrac14\big[(-3)^2+(-1)^2+0^2+1^2+3^2\big] = \tfrac14(9+1+0+1+9) = \tfrac{20}{4} = 5.\] \[\text{var}(B) = \tfrac14\big[(-1)^2+(-3)^2+0^2+1^2+3^2\big] = \tfrac14(1+9+0+1+9) = \tfrac{20}{4} = 5.\] \[\text{cov}(A,B) = \tfrac14\big[(-3)(-1)+(-1)(-3)+0+(1)(1)+(3)(3)\big] = \tfrac14(3+3+0+1+9) = \tfrac{16}{4} = 4.\] \[C = \begin{pmatrix} 5 & 4 \\ 4 & 5 \end{pmatrix}.\] Symmetric (\(C_{12}=C_{21}=4\)) ✓.

B3. \(C\) has the symmetric pattern with \(a=5\), \(b=4\): \(C(1,1) = (5+4,\ 4+5) = (9,9) = 9\,(1,1)\) and \(C(1,-1) = (5-4,\ 4-5) = (1,-1)\). Hence \(\lambda_1 = 9\) and \(\lambda_2 = 1\). Check: trace \(5+5 = 10 = 9+1\) ✓. (Bonus, not for exam: \(\det(C-\lambda I) = (5-\lambda)^2 - 16 = 0\) gives \(5-\lambda = \pm 4\), the same eigenvalues, and their product \(9 = \det C = 25-16\).)

B4. For \(\lambda_1 = 9\): \(C-9I = \begin{pmatrix}-4 & 4\\ 4 & -4\end{pmatrix}\); row 1 gives \(-4v_1+4v_2=0 \Rightarrow v_2=v_1\), so \((1,1)\). Unit version: \(\mathbf u_1 = \tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}(1,1) \approx (0.707, 0.707)\). For \(\lambda_2 = 1\): \(C-I = \begin{pmatrix}4 & 4\\ 4 & 4\end{pmatrix}\); row 1 gives \(v_1+v_2=0 \Rightarrow v_2=-v_1\), so \((1,-1)\). Unit version: \(\mathbf u_2 = \tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}(1,-1) \approx (0.707,-0.707)\). Orthogonal: \(\mathbf u_1\cdot\mathbf u_2 = \tfrac12[(1)(1)+(1)(-1)] = 0\) ✓. \(\mathbf u_1\) (largest eigenvalue, \(\lambda=9\)) is PC1; it points along the \(45^\circ\) long axis of the cloud.

B5. PC1 score of a centred sample \(\mathbf x\) is \(\mathbf x\cdot\mathbf u_1 = \tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}(x_A + x_B)\):

sample centred \((x_A,x_B)\) \(x_A+x_B\) PC1 score \(=\frac{x_A+x_B}{\sqrt2}\)
s1 \((-3,-1)\) \(-4\) \(-2.83\)
s2 \((-1,-3)\) \(-4\) \(-2.83\)
s3 \((0,0)\) \(0\) \(0\)
s4 \((1,1)\) \(2\) \(1.41\)
s5 \((3,3)\) \(6\) \(4.24\)

Sample s5 sits at the high end of PC1; s1 and s2 are tied at the low end. So PC1 orders the samples from low overall A+B expression to high.

B6. PC1 loadings are the entries of \(\mathbf u_1 = (0.707, 0.707)\) — one weight per gene. Both genes have the same loading, so PC1 is (proportional to) \(A+B\): the two genes contribute equally. Biologically this reflects that A and B rise and fall together (strong positive covariance), so the main axis of variation is their shared programme, weighting both genes alike.

B7. Total variance \(= \lambda_1+\lambda_2 = 9+1 = 10\), which equals the trace \(5+5\). \[\text{PC1}: \frac{9}{10} = 0.9 = 90\%, \qquad \text{PC2}: \frac{1}{10} = 0.1 = 10\%.\] They sum to \(1\) ✓, and the denominator \(10\) is the trace of \(C\) ✓. PC1 alone captures 90% of the variation, so a one-number-per-sample (PC1 score) summary keeps most of the signal.

B8. Variance measures spread about the mean. If the data are not centred, the sum of squares is taken about the origin, not the centroid, so the direction that “explains the most” is dominated by the offset of the whole cloud from the origin — it points roughly from the origin to the mean of the data. Centring removes that offset so the leading direction reflects the shape of the cloud (how it is stretched) rather than its location.

R1–R4. The R blocks reproduce every hand result:

X <- matrix(c(1,5, 3,3, 4,6, 5,7, 7,9), ncol = 2, byrow = TRUE)
Xc <- scale(X, center = TRUE, scale = FALSE)
C  <- cov(Xc)
C                            # matches B2:  [[5,4],[4,5]]

E <- eigen(C)
E$values                     # 9 1        -> matches B3
E$vectors                    # +/-(0.707,0.707), (0.707,-0.707)  -> matches B4 (up to sign)

scores <- Xc %*% E$vectors
scores[, 1]                  # PC1 scores -> matches B5 (up to overall sign)

E$values / sum(E$values)     # 0.9 0.1    -> matches B7
sum(E$values); sum(diag(C))  # 10 and 10  -> total variance = trace

If eigen reports the PC1 eigenvector as \((-0.707,-0.707)\), that is the same axis as \((0.707,0.707)\) with the sign flipped, and every PC1 score flips sign with it — the geometry (ordering, separation, variance explained) is unchanged.

Where this leads

  • PCA in R: prcomp does the centring, optional scaling (scale. = TRUE), and eigen-decomposition in a single call, returning scores ($x), loadings ($rotation), and standard deviations ($sdev, whose squares are the eigenvalues). Everything you computed by hand here is one function call there.
  • Scaling and the correlation matrix: running PCA with scaling is the same as eigen-decomposing the correlation matrix instead of the covariance matrix — the right choice when variables are on different units.
  • Larger data: for wide \(p \gg n\) omics matrices, PCA is computed via the singular value decomposition (SVD) of the data matrix rather than by forming a huge \(p\times p\) covariance matrix — same principal components, a faster route.
