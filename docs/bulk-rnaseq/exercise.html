Exercise: Bulk RNA-seq with DESeq2

Goal

After this exercise you will be able to:

  • Turn read counts into fractions of the RNA, and explain why a raw count means nothing without the sequencing depth.
  • Spot the composition trap: one abundant gene that goes up makes all the others look down.
  • Compute median-of-ratios size factors by hand, as DESeq2’s estimateSizeFactors does.
  • Use the rule “expect \(n\), get \(n \pm \sqrt{n}\)” to judge whether a fold change could be counting noise.
  • Simulate sequencing as drawing from a bag with sample, and draw the fan of fold changes between two replicates.
  • Run DESeq2 with a design formula on a simulated and a real dataset (airway), read log2FoldChange, lfcSE and padj, and compare with limma.
  • Use lfcShrink and explain in one sentence what it does to low-count genes.

Background

Sequencing is drawing from a bag. The library is a bag of RNA fragments. A gene that makes up a fraction \(p\) of the bag gets about \(N \cdot p\) of the \(N\) reads, but not exactly: the draw is random.

Size factors. To compare samples, divide each sample’s counts by a number that says how deep it was sequenced: its size factor. The simplest choice is the total count (counts per million, CPM, is the same times \(10^6\)). It fails when a few abundant genes change a lot: the fraction of the RNA is a zero-sum game (the composition trap).

Median of ratios (DESeq2):

  1. Leave out every gene with a 0 in any sample.
  2. For each gene, compute its geometric mean over the samples: the product of the counts, then the \(k\)-th root for \(k\) samples (for 3 samples, the cube root). This is the gene’s “typical value”.
  3. Divide every count by its gene’s geometric mean: one ratio per gene and sample.
  4. The size factor of a sample is the median of its ratios over the genes.

The median ignores the few genes that really changed. With only two samples, the ratio of the two size factors is simply the median over genes of (sample 2 / sample 1).

Counting noise. If you expect about \(n\) reads, you get \(n \pm \sqrt{n}\). The relative noise is \(\sqrt{n} / n = 1/\sqrt{n}\): 70% at \(n = 2\), 10% at 100, 3% at 1000. A fold change between two small counts is therefore very noisy. (In Error models, week 4, this noise gets its name: Poisson. Real replicates spread more, which the negative binomial adds.)

DESeq2 takes raw integer counts, a sample table and a design formula, exactly as in Design matrices in R: limma:

dds <- DESeqDataSetFromMatrix(countData = counts, colData = samples, design = ~ batch + sex + treatment)
dds <- DESeq(dds)                                    # size factors, count model per gene, tests
res <- results(dds, name = "treatment_treat_vs_ctrl")
res_shr <- lfcShrink(dds, coef = "treatment_treat_vs_ctrl", type = "apeglm")

By hand

Do these on paper. A calculator is fine.

B1. Counts and fractions. Three libraries of the same RNA, sequenced to different depths:

gene sample 1 sample 2 sample 3
A 600 1200 300
B 300 600 150
C 90 180 45
D 10 20 5

  1. Compute the total of each sample and the fraction of each gene. What do you notice?

  2. Compute counts per million (CPM) for gene D in each sample.

  3. A colleague computes sample 2 / sample 1 for every gene and reports “all genes are 2× up”. What went wrong?

  4. Gene G has 512 reads in a library of 10 million reads, and 512 reads in another library of 40 million. In which library is G a larger fraction of the RNA, and by what factor?

B2. The composition trap. In sample 1, gene A makes up half of the RNA. In sample 2, the cells make 7 times more A; nothing else changes. Both samples are sequenced to 2000 reads:

gene sample 1 sample 2
A 1000 1750
B 500 125
C 300 75
D 200 50

  1. Compute the fraction of each gene in each sample, and the fold change of the fractions (sample 2 / sample 1).

  2. Which genes look “down”? Did they really go down? What fold change do you get for A, and what is the truth?

  3. Compute the ratio sample 2 / sample 1 for each gene, and take the median. Divide sample 2 by this number. What are the fold changes now?

B3. Median of ratios. Five genes in three samples:

gene s1 s2 s3
A 100 200 400
B 50 100 200
C 30 60 120
D 100 200 3200
E 8 0 32

  1. Which gene is left out, and why?

  2. Compute the geometric mean of each remaining gene. (For gene A: \(100 \cdot 200 \cdot 400 = 8\,000\,000\), and the cube root of that is 200.)

  3. Divide each count by its gene’s geometric mean, and take the median per sample. These are the size factors. (With four genes, the median is the mean of the middle two.)

  4. Divide the counts of genes A and D by the size factors. Which gene changed, in which sample, and by how much?

  5. Now use the totals of the samples as size factors instead. Compute the totals, and the fraction of gene A in s1 and s3. What would you conclude about gene A, and why is it wrong?

B4. Which fold changes would you trust? A control and a treated sample, same depth, one sample each:

gene control treated
A 1000 1100
B 400 1600
C 2 8
D 1 8
E 3 0
F 900 225
G 100 50

  1. Compute the log2 fold change (treated / control) of each gene, and rank the genes by its size (largest first, ignoring the sign).

  2. Suppose a gene did not change. Then both counts come from the same expected value \(n\), roughly their mean. For each gene, compute \(n\) and \(\sqrt{n}\), and how many \(\sqrt{n}\) the counts are away from \(n\).

  3. Which genes would you believe are really changed? Which of your top 3 from (a) are likely noise?

  4. What would you need, in the experiment, to say more about gene G?

Explore: noise in a ratio of counts

The demo draws 2000 pairs of replicate counts with the same expected value \(n\) (buttons from 1 to 1000). Panel (a) is a histogram of the log2 ratio of each pair; pairs with a zero get their own bars (0, \(\infty\), 0/0), and pairs at least 2-fold apart are red. Its header shows the spread of the counts against \(\sqrt{n}\) and the % of pairs at least 2-fold apart. Panel (b) is a fan of 2000 genes with averages from 0.5 to 5000; red circles mark the 10 largest fold changes.

E1. Click through \(n\) = 1, 2, 5, 10, 30 and 100. Note the % of pairs at least 2-fold apart each time. At \(n = 1\) and 2, how many pairs contain a zero, and what is their ratio?

E2. At \(n = 100\) and \(n = 1000\), compare the spread of the counts with \(\sqrt{n}\) in the header. By how much must \(n\) grow to halve the relative spread? Then tick add biological variation: what happens at \(n = 1000\)?

E3. In the fan (b), what do the 10 largest fold changes (red circles) have in common? Compare the % of genes at least 2-fold apart below an average of 10 and above 100. What does that mean for a gene list sorted by fold change?

Solutions

E1. At the defaults: 77% at \(n = 1\), 65% at 2, 37% at 5, 17% at 10, 1.3% at 30 and 0% at 100. Nothing changed between the replicates, yet at low counts most pairs look like a 2-fold change or more. At \(n = 1\) and 2 many pairs contain a zero: their ratio is 0, \(\infty\) or 0/0, so their log2 ratio is \(-\infty\), \(+\infty\) or undefined. (That is also why the SD in the header, computed on zero-free pairs only, is not a good summary there.)

E2. The spread of the counts is close to \(\sqrt{n}\): about 10 at \(n = 100\), about 32 at \(n = 1000\). The relative spread goes as \(1/\sqrt{n}\), so \(n\) must grow 4 times to halve it. With biological variation on, the spread at \(n = 1000\) no longer goes to zero: the SD of the log2 ratio stays around 0.47 and about 3% of pairs are still 2-fold apart. Real replicates differ by more than counting noise; Error models (week 4) adds this with the negative binomial.

E3. The 10 largest fold changes all belong to low-count genes (averages of about 3 to 5.5). Below an average of 10, 56% of the genes are at least 2-fold apart; above 100, none are. A list sorted by raw fold change is topped by noise; you need a test that knows the count (DESeq2’s lfcSE and padj), and a shrunken fold change for ranking (R4).

In R

Base R is enough for R1 and R2. R3 and R4 need DESeq2, limma, apeglm and airway: install them once with BiocManager::install(c("DESeq2", "limma", "apeglm", "airway")) (see Install R).

R1 — Sequencing as drawing from a bag

set.seed(1)
G     <- 2000                                  # number of genes
genes <- paste0("g", 1:G)
p     <- exp(rnorm(G, 0, 2))                   # some genes are rare, some very common
p     <- p / sum(p)                            # the fraction of the bag for each gene
N     <- 1e6                                   # reads per sample
expected <- N * p                              # expected reads per gene

draw_library <- function() {
  reads <- sample(genes, N, replace = TRUE, prob = p)    # N fragments drawn at random
  as.vector(table(factor(reads, levels = genes)))        # count them, keep the zeros
}
rep1 <- draw_library()                         # two replicates: same bag, same depth
rep2 <- draw_library()

  1. Look at head(cbind(expected, rep1, rep2)). Do the counts match the expected values? Which genes match best, relative to their size?

  2. How many genes have 0 reads in rep1? Compute lfc <- log2(rep2 / rep1). How many values are not finite (!is.finite(lfc)), and why?

  3. Draw the fan: m <- (rep1 + rep2) / 2; plot(m, lfc, log = "x"). Add abline(h = 0). Describe the shape.

  4. Nothing changed between the replicates. Still: list the 10 genes with the largest abs(lfc) (among the finite ones), with their counts. How many genes look at least “2-fold changed” (abs(lfc) > 1), and what is the largest mean count among them? Among genes with a mean count of at least 100, what is the largest abs(lfc)?

R2 — The \(n \pm \sqrt{n}\) rule

Repeat the draw 100 times and look at the spread of each gene. tabulate(sample.int(...)) does the same as R1’s draw_library(), only faster; it still takes about 20 seconds.

draw_fast <- function() tabulate(sample.int(G, N, replace = TRUE, prob = p), nbins = G)
reps <- replicate(100, draw_fast())            # genes x 100 repeated libraries
mu <- rowMeans(reps)                           # mean count of each gene
s  <- apply(reps, 1, sd)                       # its standard deviation

  1. Find the genes whose expected count is closest to 2, 100 and 1000 (which.min(abs(expected - 100))). For each, compare s with sqrt(mu).

  2. Plot the variance against the mean on log–log axes: plot(mu[mu > 0], s[mu > 0]^2, log = "xy"); abline(0, 1). What does the line \(y = x\) say about the variance?

  3. Compute the relative noise s / mu for the three genes in (a) and compare with \(1/\sqrt{n}\). How many reads must a gene have for 10% relative noise? For 1%?

R3 — DESeq2 on the simulated experiment from covariates-r

This is the simulation from Design matrices in R: limma: 2000 genes, 12 samples, batch unbalanced against treatment, and 200 genes that truly respond to treatment. Run it, and the limma analysis, so you can compare.

library(limma)
set.seed(1)

samples <- data.frame(
  batch     = factor(rep(c("A", "B"), each = 6)),
  sex       = factor(rep(c("F", "M"), times = 6)),
  treatment = factor(c("ctrl", rep("treat", 5), rep("ctrl", 5), "treat")))
rownames(samples) <- paste0("s", 1:12)

n <- 2000
lfc_treat <- rep(0, n); lfc_treat[1:200]   <- sample(c(-1.5, 1.5), 200, TRUE)
lfc_batch <- rep(0, n); lfc_batch[101:600] <- sample(c(-2, 2), 500, TRUE)
lfc_sex   <- rep(0, n); lfc_sex[1901:1950] <- 3
B <- cbind(lfc_batch, lfc_sex, lfc_treat)

X    <- model.matrix(~ batch + sex + treatment, data = samples)
base <- exp(rnorm(n, log(200), 1))              # baseline mean count of each gene
mu   <- base * 2^(B %*% t(X[, -1]))             # mean count: genes x samples
phi  <- 0.05 + 2 / base                         # negative-binomial dispersion
counts <- matrix(rnbinom(n * 12, mu = mu, size = 1 / phi), nrow = n,
                 dimnames = list(paste0("g", 1:n), rownames(samples)))
truth_de <- lfc_treat != 0                      # TRUE for the 200 DE genes

# limma, as in covariates-r
v   <- voom(counts, X)
tt  <- topTable(eBayes(lmFit(v, X)), coef = "treatmenttreat", number = Inf)
table(limma_called = tt[rownames(counts), "adj.P.Val"] < 0.05, truly_DE = truth_de)

Now DESeq2 on the same counts:

library(DESeq2)
dds <- DESeqDataSetFromMatrix(countData = counts, colData = samples,
                              design = ~ batch + sex + treatment)
dds <- DESeq(dds)
resultsNames(dds)
res <- results(dds, name = "treatment_treat_vs_ctrl")
table(called = res$padj < 0.05, truly_DE = truth_de, useNA = "ifany")

  1. How does DESeq2 name the coefficients? Why is it safer to ask for one by name =?

  2. Compare the number of true and false calls with limma. Why are some padj values NA?

  3. Look at sizeFactors(dds). Every sample was simulated with the same depth. Are the size factors close to 1? Check one by hand: median(counts[, 1] / exp(rowMeans(log(counts))), na.rm = TRUE) (genes with a 0 give NaN or 0 and need care: which genes does DESeq2 leave out?).

  4. Plot plot(res$baseMean, res$lfcSE, log = "xy"). Where is lfcSE largest? Relate it to the fan in R1.

  5. What does DESeqDataSetFromMatrix(counts, samples_conf, design = ~ batch + treatment) say, with the confounded samples_conf from covariates-r R5 (samples_conf <- samples; samples_conf$treatment <- factor(ifelse(samples$batch == "A", "ctrl", "treat")))?

R4 — A real dataset: airway

The airway package has RNA-seq of four airway smooth-muscle cell lines (cell), each untreated or treated with dexamethasone (dex), a glucocorticoid.

library(DESeq2)
library(airway)
data(airway)
levels(airway$dex)
airway$dex <- relevel(airway$dex, ref = "untrt")
dds_air <- DESeqDataSet(airway, design = ~ cell + dex)
dds_air <- dds_air[rowSums(counts(dds_air)) >= 10, ]   # drop almost-empty genes
dds_air <- DESeq(dds_air)
res_air <- results(dds_air, name = "dex_trt_vs_untrt")
sum(res_air$padj < 0.05, na.rm = TRUE)

  1. What are the levels of dex before relevel? What would have gone wrong without it?

  2. Compare sizeFactors(dds_air) with the total counts scaled the same way: colSums(counts(dds_air)) / exp(mean(log(colSums(counts(dds_air))))). Are the two close? Which samples were sequenced deepest?

  3. Why is cell in the design? Compare the number of genes at padj < 0.05 with design = ~ dex.

  4. Draw plotMA(res_air, ylim = c(-5, 5)). Where do you see the fan? Where are the called genes (in colour)?

  5. Shrink the fold changes and draw the MA plot again:

res_shr <- lfcShrink(dds_air, coef = "dex_trt_vs_untrt", type = "apeglm")
plotMA(res_shr, ylim = c(-5, 5))
top_raw <- head(res_air[order(-abs(res_air$log2FoldChange)), c("baseMean", "log2FoldChange", "padj")], 10)
top_shr <- head(res_shr[order(-abs(res_shr$log2FoldChange)), c("baseMean", "log2FoldChange", "padj")], 10)

What changed in the plot? Compare baseMean in top_raw and top_shr. Which list would you rather send to a colleague who wants “the genes most changed by dexamethasone”?

  1. Draw a volcano plot: the shrunken fold change against \(-\log_{10}\) padj.
ok  <- !is.na(res_shr$padj)
big <- ok & res_shr$padj < 0.05 & abs(res_shr$log2FoldChange) > 1
plot(res_shr$log2FoldChange[ok], -log10(res_shr$padj[ok]), pch = 16, cex = 0.4,
     col = ifelse(big[ok], "red", "grey"), xlab = "log2 fold change", ylab = "-log10(padj)")
abline(h = -log10(0.05), lty = 2); abline(v = c(-1, 1), lty = 3)
sum(big); sum(big & res_shr$log2FoldChange > 0)

How many genes are at padj < 0.05 and changed more than 2-fold, and how many of them go up? Find SPARCL1, FKBP5 and ALOX15B (rowData(dds_air)$gene_name) and explain where each sits.

Hints

Hint 1 — fractions and CPM (B1, B2)

Fraction = count / total of that sample. CPM = fraction \(\times 10^6\). A fold change of fractions is (fraction in sample 2) / (fraction in sample 1). The fractions of one sample always add up to 1.

Hint 2 — median of ratios (B2c, B3)

Follow the four steps in the Background. A geometric mean of 3 numbers is the cube root of their product; when the counts in a row are \(a\), \(2a\), \(4a\), the product is \(8a^3\) and the geometric mean is \(2a\). The median of an even number of values is the mean of the middle two. See estimateSizeFactors and the DESeq2 vignette, section “Normalization”.

Hint 3 — log2 fold changes and the \(\sqrt{n}\) rule (B4)

\(\log_2 4 = 2\), \(\log_2 8 = 3\), \(\log_2 0.25 = -2\), and \(\log_2 1.1 \approx 0.14\). For “how many \(\sqrt{n}\) away”: \((\text{count} - n) / \sqrt{n}\). More than about 3 is hard to get from counting noise alone; 1 or 2 is common.

Hint 4 — simulating the bag (R1, R2)

sample(genes, N, replace = TRUE, prob = p) draws \(N\) gene names, each with probability \(p\). table counts them; turning the draws into a factor with all genes as levels keeps the genes with 0 reads. tabulate does the same for numbers 1 to G. replicate repeats a call and puts the results side by side as columns.

Hint 5 — DESeq2 (R3, R4)

DESeqDataSetFromMatrix takes raw integer counts, the sample table and the design. DESeq fits every gene. results(dds, name = ...) picks a coefficient by its name from resultsNames(dds); without name it reports the last variable of the formula. padj is NA for genes DESeq2 does not test (too few reads, or an extreme outlier); see the vignette’s section “Independent filtering”. plotMA draws the fold change against baseMean.

Hint 6 — shrinkage (R4e)

lfcShrink with type = "apeglm" needs the apeglm package and a coef name from resultsNames. It changes log2FoldChange and lfcSE, not the test.

Solutions

Solutions — by hand

B1. (a) Totals 1000, 2000 and 500. The fractions are the same in all three samples: A 0.6, B 0.3, C 0.09, D 0.01. The samples differ only in depth.

  1. Gene D: \(10 / 1000 \times 10^6 = 10\,000\) CPM in sample 1, \(20 / 2000 \times 10^6 = 10\,000\) in sample 2, \(5 / 500 \times 10^6 = 10\,000\) in sample 3. The same.

  2. Sample 2 was sequenced twice as deep, so every count doubled. The ratio of raw counts measures the depth, not the biology. Divide by the size factors (here the totals) first.

  3. \(512 / 10\) million \(= 51.2\) per million, and \(512 / 40\) million \(= 12.8\) per million. G is a 4 times larger fraction of the RNA in the first library.

B2. (a)

gene fraction 1 fraction 2 fold change
A 0.50 0.875 1.75
B 0.25 0.0625 0.25
C 0.15 0.0375 0.25
D 0.10 0.025 0.25

  1. B, C and D look 4 times down (log2 FC \(-2\)), but nothing happened to them. A looks 1.75 times up, but truly went up 7 times. In the tube, sample 2 had \(7000 + 500 + 300 + 200 = 8000\) units of RNA for every 2000 in sample 1, so each read of sample 2 stands for 4 times as much RNA: A took most of the reads.

  2. The ratios are 1.75, 0.25, 0.25, 0.25; the median is 0.25. Sample 2 divided by 0.25: A 7000, B 500, C 300, D 200. Now B, C and D are unchanged and A is 7 times up: the truth.

B3. (a) Gene E: it has a 0 in s2, so its geometric mean is 0 and its ratios are useless (\(8/0\)).

  1. A: \(\sqrt[3]{8\,000\,000} = 200\). B: \(\sqrt[3]{1\,000\,000} = 100\). C: \(\sqrt[3]{216\,000} = 60\). D: \(\sqrt[3]{64\,000\,000} = 400\).

gene s1 s2 s3
A 0.5 1 2
B 0.5 1 2
C 0.5 1 2
D 0.25 0.5 8
median 0.5 1 2

The size factors are 0.5, 1 and 2: s3 was sequenced 4 times deeper than s1.

  1. A: \(100/0.5 = 200\), \(200/1 = 200\), \(400/2 = 200\). Unchanged. D: \(200\), \(200\), \(3200/2 = 1600\): 8 times up in s3. The median was not fooled by D, because only one gene in four changed.

  2. Totals: 288, 560 and 3952. Fraction of A: \(100/288 = 0.35\) in s1, \(400/3952 = 0.10\) in s3. A would look about 3.4 times down in s3. It is wrong: D took a big fraction of s3’s reads (3200 of 3952), so every other gene’s fraction fell. That is the composition trap.

Where this leads

  • Error models (week 4): the counting noise of R2 is the Poisson distribution (variance = mean), and the extra spread between biological replicates is the negative binomial, the count model inside DESeq2.
  • Why penalize?, Bayesian vs frequentist and Hierarchical models (week 4): lfcShrink is a penalty on large fold changes, a prior that says “most genes change little”, and information shared across genes. These units explain how.
  • Log and pseudolog transforms (optional): the pseudocount table there shows the same noise of small counts, seen through a log.
