Bayesian vs frequentist

Week 4 — Bayesian vs frequentist

Quantitative biology and integrative omics

About this lecture

Your earlier statistics was almost certainly frequentist: fixed (but unknown) parameters, p-values, confidence intervals. Today we meet the other big framework — Bayesian reasoning — and see how they differ.

  • Probability as a degree of belief, updated by data — not just a long-run frequency.
  • Bayes’ rule in one line: \text{posterior} \propto \text{prior} \times \text{likelihood}.
  • Frequentist vs Bayesian: fixed parameters + p-values vs distributions of belief + credible intervals.
  • One worked biology example: estimating a proportion (fraction of cells expressing a marker) with a prior.
  • Why a biologist might want this: honest uncertainty, using prior knowledge, and a natural fit for hierarchy.

This is a concept and intuition day. The maths stays light; the goal is to think in the Bayesian way and read a posterior.

The one message for today: there are two coherent ways to do statistics, and the students have only seen one of them. Frequentist statistics treats the parameter as a fixed unknown and asks “how surprising is my data?”; Bayesian statistics treats the parameter as uncertain and asks “what should I now believe about it?”. Everything else — Bayes’ rule, priors, posteriors, credible intervals — hangs off that shift in what “probability” means. Keep it conceptual: there is no algebra on the main slides. The two demos (five guesses for the infection rate, then the cell-counting curves) carry the ideas, and the only arithmetic is adding counts. Tell them the skill to practise is computing a small posterior by hand and reading a credible interval, not deriving anything; the formulas behind the update are on one optional slide at the end.

A biology picture first

You stain a population of cells for a marker and count, under the microscope, how many are positive. Out of 20 cells, 6 are positive.

  • Your instinct (frequentist): the fraction is \hat p = 6/20 = 0.30. Done.
  • But you have only 20 cells. How sure are you? Could the true fraction be 0.2? 0.45?
  • And you are not starting from nothing: in three previous experiments this marker sat around 20%. Should that count?

Frequentist analysis gives you a single number plus a confidence interval and ignores the earlier experiments. Bayesian analysis gives you a full distribution of belief over p and lets you fold in what you already knew.

Anchor everything in this one scene: counting positive cells. The frequentist reflex is p-hat = 6/20 = 0.3, full stop. Push on two discomforts a biologist actually feels: (1) 20 cells is a small sample, so how confident are we, and (2) we ran this before and have prior knowledge — throwing it away feels wasteful. Those two discomforts are exactly what the Bayesian machinery addresses: a whole posterior distribution instead of a point, and a principled way to include the prior. Do not write any formulas yet; just seed the motivation.

Two meanings of “probability”

The split between the two schools comes from what the word probability even means.

Frequentist Bayesian
Probability is… a long-run frequency a degree of belief
The parameter p is… fixed, just unknown uncertain — described by a distribution
Data is… random (repeatable) the fixed thing you actually saw
A result is… a point estimate + CI, a p-value a posterior distribution

Frequentist question: “If p were really 0.2, how surprising is my data?” Bayesian question: “Given my data, what should I now believe about p?”

This is the philosophical fork, and it is worth stating slowly. Frequentists reserve “probability” for things that can be repeated — coin flips, cells sampled — so they will never write P(p = 0.3): the parameter is fixed, not random, so it has no probability distribution. Bayesians allow “probability” to describe uncertainty/belief about anything, including a fixed-but-unknown parameter, so P(p) is perfectly meaningful. That single move is what lets Bayesians put a distribution on the parameter and update it. Everything downstream (priors, posteriors, credible intervals) follows. Emphasise the two contrasting questions at the bottom — they are the cleanest one-line summary.

Bayes’ rule

In one sentence:

What you believe after the data = what you believed before × how well each possibility explains the data — then rescaled so the beliefs add up to 1.

The four pieces, for a hypothesis H (a value of p) and the data D you saw:

  • prior P(H): what you believed about H before the data.
  • likelihood P(D \mid H): how probable the data is if H were true.
  • posterior P(H \mid D): your updated belief after seeing the data.
  • evidence P(D): the total of all the products; dividing by it makes the posterior add up to 1.

In symbols: P(H \mid D) = P(D \mid H)\,P(H)\,/\,P(D).

Lead with the sentence, not the formula: the formula is just a compact way of writing it. Reassure them this is not exotic: Bayes’ rule is a one-line consequence of P(A and B) = P(A|B)P(B) = P(B|A)P(A), which they may have seen. The novelty is not the algebra, it is applying it with H = “a value of the parameter” and D = “the data”. Name each of the four pieces slowly and in plain English. The evidence P(D) is nothing mysterious: it is the sum of prior × likelihood over all hypotheses, the number that makes the posterior add to 1. The next slide lets them do exactly this with five hypotheses and see each step as bars.

Try it: Bayes’ rule on five guesses

Five possible infection rates; 2 of 5 cells infected. Prior × likelihood, add up, divide.

Each hypothesis (infection rate 0.1 to 0.5) gets a prior bar; they start equal (flat prior). The likelihood row is how probable “2 infected out of 5” is under each rate. Multiply prior by likelihood, add up the products (the total is the evidence P(D)), and divide each by the total so the posterior sums to 1. Press “step by step” and click through the four steps. With the flat prior the posterior just follows the likelihood: 0.4 comes out on top (about 0.28), 0.3 and 0.5 close behind, 0.1 almost ruled out (about 0.06). Now drag the prior: put most of the weight on 0.1 and see how much the data can still pull it. Add positives and negatives with the buttons: with more cells the data outweigh the prior. Finally, “posterior → new prior” and add more data: updating twice with half the data gives the same answer as updating once with all of it. Tick “show the arithmetic” if someone wants the numbers in a table.

Posterior \propto prior \times likelihood

The denominator P(D) does not depend on H — it is the same for every hypothesis. So the shape of the posterior is fixed entirely by the numerator:

\underbrace{P(H \mid D)}_{\text{posterior}} \ \propto\ \underbrace{P(D \mid H)}_{\text{likelihood}} \times \underbrace{P(H)}_{\text{prior}}

(“\propto” means “proportional to” — equal up to a constant that we fix at the end so the total probability is 1.)

This is the sentence to memorise:

Posterior belief = prior belief, reweighted by how well each hypothesis explains the data.

Where the data is strong, the likelihood dominates and the prior barely matters. Where the data is weak (few cells!), the prior still has a say.

This is the single most useful line in the lecture — put it on a card. In the demo the “divide by the total” step was the last one; it only rescales, so the shape of the posterior is already there after multiplying. Stress the interpretation at the bottom: the prior is your starting belief, the likelihood is the data’s verdict on each hypothesis, and multiplying them reweights the prior toward hypotheses that explain the data well. The figure is the same as the demo, but with every value of p between 0 and 1 instead of five bars. Then the crucial practical point for biologists: with lots of data the likelihood swamps the prior (Bayesian and frequentist answers converge), but with little data (our 20 cells) the prior genuinely influences the answer — which is a feature, not a bug, if the prior is honest.

Our example: a belief curve for p

Estimate p = the true fraction of marker-positive cells. Data: k = 6 positive out of n = 20.

  • Likelihood: for each possible p, how probable is “6 positive out of 20”? It is highest at p = 6/20 = 0.30. (In R: dbinom(6, 20, p).)
  • Prior: a curve over p from 0 to 1. We use a Beta(\alpha, \beta) curve: read it as a belief worth \alpha imagined positive cells and \beta imagined negative cells.

Set up the two ingredients concretely, in words. The likelihood is the same as in the five-guesses demo, only now for every p between 0 and 1: for each p, how probable is it to see 6 positives in 20 cells (the binomial count from earlier statistics; dbinom in R). The prior needs to live on [0,1] because p is a proportion, and the Beta family is the standard choice there. Do not write its formula; give them the reading instead: alpha imagined positives and beta imagined negatives. Beta(1,1) is one of each, almost nothing: a flat curve, no prior opinion. Beta(2,8) is like having seen 2 positives in 10 cells before: it leans toward 0.2, our earlier experiments. Beta(8,4) leans large. The more imagined cells, the narrower and more confident the curve. Treat Beta as the go-to curve for a fraction, the way the Gaussian is the go-to for an unbounded quantity. The formulas are on the optional slide at the end.

Updating = adding counts

With a Beta prior and counted cells, prior × likelihood is another Beta curve, and the update is just tallying:

Add your positives to \alpha, and your negatives to \beta.

\text{prior Beta}(\alpha, \beta) \quad\longrightarrow\quad \text{posterior Beta}(\alpha + k,\ \beta + n - k)

The imagined cells of the prior and the real cells of the experiment are simply pooled. A prior with few imagined cells is soon outnumbered by the data; one with many imagined cells takes a lot of data to move.

This is the one rule they need, and it is pure arithmetic: positives go to alpha, negatives go to beta. The reason it works (multiplying the two formulas just adds exponents, the Beta is “conjugate” to the binomial) is on the optional slide at the end; do not derive it here. The pseudo-count reading makes it stick: alpha and beta are imagined positives and negatives; the data just adds its real positives and negatives on top. Beta(1,1) = 1 imagined positive + 1 imagined negative = barely any prior weight, so the data dominates. This lucky pairing is special; in general the posterior has no formula and we sample it (MCMC) — mention only as a pointer.

Turning the crank, two priors

Data: k = 6, n - k = 14.

Uniform prior Beta(1,1) — “no opinion”: \text{posterior} = \text{Beta}(1+6,\ 1+14) = \text{Beta}(7, 15), \quad \text{mean } \tfrac{7}{22} \approx 0.318

Informative prior Beta(2,8) — centred near the earlier \sim 20\%: \text{posterior} = \text{Beta}(2+6,\ 8+14) = \text{Beta}(8, 22), \quad \text{mean } \tfrac{8}{30} \approx 0.267

(The mean of \text{Beta}(\alpha,\beta) is \alpha/(\alpha+\beta): the share of positives among all cells, imagined and real.)

The informative prior pulls the estimate down toward the earlier experiments — and with only 20 cells, that pull is real. Collect 200 cells and both posteriors would sit almost on top of the data.

Now make it numeric and concrete. Two priors, same data, worked in one line each with the tally rule. The uniform-prior posterior mean 0.318 is basically the data estimate 0.30 (nudged slightly by the +1,+1 pseudo-counts). The informative-prior posterior mean 0.267 sits between the prior’s 0.2 and the data’s 0.3 — a compromise weighted by how much information each carries. Hammer the small-sample point: with n=20 the prior’s few pseudo-counts are a meaningful fraction of the total, so they move the answer; with n=200 the data’s 200 counts swamp the prior and both posteriors collapse onto ~0.30. This is the honest behaviour we want: priors matter exactly when data is scarce, and fade away when data is plentiful.

Credible interval vs confidence interval

Bayesian uncertainty comes straight from the posterior. A 95% credible interval is the range holding 95% of the area under the posterior curve: for Beta(7,15), from about 0.15 to 0.52.

You are allowed to say the honest sentence: “there is a 95% probability that p lies in this interval.”

A frequentist 95% confidence interval does not mean that. It means: if you repeated the whole experiment many times, 95% of the intervals so constructed would contain the true fixed p. Any single interval either contains p or does not — no probability attached.

Credible interval Confidence interval
Statement about the parameter the procedure
“95% probability p is inside” ✓ allowed ✗ not allowed

This is the contrast students most often get wrong, so slow down. The credible interval is a direct probability statement about the parameter — legal for a Bayesian because p has a distribution. The confidence interval is a statement about the long-run behaviour of the interval-construction procedure, NOT about any single interval; strictly, a given 95% CI has already either caught p or missed it, with no probability. Most scientists secretly interpret their confidence intervals as credible intervals — the Bayesian version is the interpretation people actually want, made legitimate. The endpoints come from qbeta on Beta(7,15) (R slide below). The next slide shows what “repeated many times” looks like.

p-value vs posterior probability

Suppose you care whether the true fraction exceeds 25%.

  • Frequentist: set up H_0: p = 0.25, compute a p-value = probability of data at least this extreme if H_0 were true. A small p-value means “the data would be surprising under H_0.” It does not tell you the probability that H_0 is true.
  • Bayesian: just read it off the posterior. P(p > 0.25 \mid \text{data}) is the shaded area under the posterior to the right of 0.25: for Beta(7,15) about 0.74. A direct probability that the hypothesis is true, given your data.

The Bayesian answers the question people think a p-value answers.

Reinforce the deepest and most common misreading in all of applied statistics: a p-value is P(data this extreme | H0), NOT P(H0 | data). It measures surprise under the null, not the probability the null is true. The Bayesian posterior gives you exactly P(hypothesis | data), the quantity everyone actually wants. Once you have the posterior Beta(7,15), a hypothesis like “p > 0.25” is just an area under the curve: 0.74 of the area lies to the right of 0.25 (one pbeta call in R; the demo on the next slide shades it). Do not turn this into a p-value-bashing rant; the point is that the two frameworks answer genuinely different questions, and the Bayesian question is often the scientifically natural one.

The same computation in R

Everything above is a couple of built-in functions. (Static examples — nothing to install.)

k <- 6; n <- 20

# Frequentist point estimate
p_hat <- k / n                     # 0.30

# Bayesian update with a uniform prior Beta(1, 1)
a0 <- 1; b0 <- 1
a  <- a0 + k                       # 7   (prior successes + observed)
b  <- b0 + (n - k)                 # 15  (prior failures  + observed)

a / (a + b)                        # posterior mean            -> 0.318
qbeta(c(0.025, 0.975), a, b)       # 95% credible interval     -> ~0.15 .. 0.52
1 - pbeta(0.25, a, b)              # P(p > 0.25 | data)        -> ~0.74
# An informative prior Beta(2, 8) (earlier experiments, ~20% positive)
a0 <- 2; b0 <- 8
a  <- a0 + k                       # 8
b  <- b0 + (n - k)                 # 22
a / (a + b)                        # posterior mean            -> 0.267

Show that the whole lecture reduces to arithmetic plus qbeta/pbeta — no special package, no MCMC, because the tally rule gives the posterior directly. Walk the first block: a and b are the updated Beta parameters, a/(a+b) is the posterior mean, qbeta gives credible-interval endpoints (the quantiles), and 1 - pbeta(0.25, a, b) is the posterior probability that p exceeds 0.25. The second block just swaps the prior and re-runs the same two lines. Emphasise these are static illustrations (no packages are installed at render time); students run them in their own session. The exercise has them reproduce these numbers and vary the prior.

Why a biologist might go Bayesian

  • Honest uncertainty. You get a full posterior, not a lone point estimate — and a credible interval you are actually allowed to interpret as “where the parameter probably is.”
  • Use what you already know. Prior experiments, known biology, or literature values enter formally through the prior instead of being ignored or hand-waved.
  • Small samples behave sensibly. With few cells the prior gently regularises the estimate; with many cells the data takes over automatically.
  • Natural hierarchy. Genes within pathways, cells within patients, patients within cohorts — Bayesian hierarchical models let lower levels “borrow strength” from the group, which is exactly how modern tools shrink noisy per-gene estimates in differential expression.

Sell the practical upside, tied to omics. Honest uncertainty: the posterior and credible interval say what scientists actually mean. Prior knowledge: biology is rarely a blank slate, and the prior is the formal slot for what you already know (used carefully). Small samples: the prior acts as principled regularisation, so a gene seen in three cells does not get a wild estimate. Hierarchy is the big one for genomics: hierarchical/partial-pooling models let a noisy per-gene estimate borrow strength from the distribution of all genes — this is exactly the empirical-Bayes shrinkage inside tools like limma and DESeq2 (DESeq2’s lfcShrink, met in Bulk RNA-seq with DESeq2, is one example), even if their internals are not taught here. Keep it as motivation, not a how-to.

Being fair to the frequentist

Bayesian is not automatically “better.” Trade-offs:

  • The prior is a choice, and a bad or overconfident prior biases the answer. You must be able to defend it — and check how much it matters (sensitivity analysis).
  • Frequentist methods are fast, standard, and prior-free — for a large, clean dataset the two usually agree, and a t-test is simpler than a model.
  • Computation. Only lucky cases (like today’s Beta prior with counted cells) give a formula; realistic models need MCMC sampling — more machinery, more that can go wrong.
  • Reproducibility. A frequentist pipeline has fewer subjective knobs, which some fields prefer.

Rule of thumb: lots of clean data → they agree; little data, strong prior knowledge, or nested structure → Bayesian earns its keep.

Guard against the common student overcorrection (“Bayesian is just correct and p-values are wrong”). Both frameworks are coherent and useful. The prior is a real responsibility: a sharp prior in the wrong place will drag the answer, so honesty demands reporting it and doing a sensitivity check (does the conclusion survive a weaker prior?). Frequentist tools are prior-free, fast, and standard, and with abundant data the posterior is dominated by the likelihood so both schools converge anyway. The cost of going Bayesian in realistic models is computational — MCMC and its diagnostics. Give them the practical rule of thumb at the bottom as the decision heuristic. This mirrors the course’s “honest about what we defer/assume” principle.

Optional: the maths behind the Beta update

For readers who like formulas; nothing in the deck depends on this slide.

  • Likelihood (binomial): P(k \mid p) = \binom{n}{k}\, p^{k}(1-p)^{n-k} \ \propto\ p^{k}(1-p)^{n-k} — for our data p^{6}(1-p)^{14}.
  • Prior (Beta): P(p) \propto p^{\alpha-1}(1-p)^{\beta-1}.
  • Multiply — the exponents add: p^{\alpha-1}(1-p)^{\beta-1}\cdot p^{k}(1-p)^{n-k} = p^{(\alpha+k)-1}(1-p)^{(\beta+n-k)-1}, which is again a Beta shape: Beta(\alpha+k,\ \beta+n-k). A prior that gives a posterior of the same family is called conjugate.
  • Tail area: P(p > 0.25 \mid \text{data}) = \int_{0.25}^{1} \text{Beta}(p;\,7,15)\, dp \approx 0.74, which is 1 - pbeta(0.25, 7, 15) in R.

Optional, for the mathematically curious; skip it in a normal lecture. The binomial coefficient is dropped in the proportionality because it does not involve p. Multiplying the two shapes just adds exponents, so the posterior is again a Beta, with the counts added: this is why the tally rule works. “Conjugate” is the name for such a matching prior-likelihood pair; it makes the update pure arithmetic. The integral is the shaded area from the demo, written as a formula; pbeta computes it.
