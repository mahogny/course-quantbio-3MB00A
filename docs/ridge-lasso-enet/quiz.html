Quiz: ridge, lasso & elastic net

Short questions to test your understanding of penalized linear models. Try each on paper first, then open the answer. Several are written in the style of the on-campus exam (no computer).

Q1. Why does ordinary least squares fail (or become unstable) when you have 20 000 genes and 40 samples?

Hint

Count unknowns versus equations, and ask whether \(X^\top X\) can be inverted.

Answer

There are far more unknown coefficients (\(p = 20\,000\)) than samples (\(n = 40\)), so the system is underdetermined — infinitely many \(\boldsymbol\beta\) fit perfectly, and OLS picks unstable, noise-fitting coefficients. Equivalently \(X^\top X\) has rank at most \(n < p\), so it is singular and \((X^\top X)^{-1}\) does not exist. This is the \(p \gg n\) regime.

Q2. Write the general penalized objective, and state the penalty term for ridge and for lasso.

Hint

Fit term plus \(\lambda\) times a penalty. Squares vs. absolute values.

Answer

\[\hat{\boldsymbol\beta} = \arg\min_{\boldsymbol\beta}\ \lVert\mathbf{y}-X\boldsymbol\beta\rVert^2 + \lambda\cdot\text{penalty}(\boldsymbol\beta).\] Ridge (L2): penalty \(= \sum_j \beta_j^2\). Lasso (L1): penalty \(= \sum_j \lvert\beta_j\rvert\).

Q3. What is the single most important behavioural difference between ridge and lasso?

Hint

What happens to a coefficient that is not useful?

Answer

Lasso sets some coefficients exactly to zero (dropping those predictors — automatic feature selection, a sparse model). Ridge shrinks all coefficients toward zero but keeps them all nonzero. So lasso selects; ridge only shrinks.

Q4. In the constraint-geometry picture, the ridge budget region is a circle and the lasso region is a diamond. Why does that difference produce zero coefficients for lasso but not for ridge?

Hint

Where are the corners of the diamond, and what does the RSS ellipse tend to touch first?

Answer

The diamond has corners that lie on the axes, where one coordinate is exactly zero. The expanding RSS ellipse tends to first touch a pointy corner, so the lasso solution often lands with a coefficient exactly \(0\). The circle is smooth with no corners, so ridge’s touch point sits at a generic location with all coefficients nonzero.

Q5. What does the ridge closed form \((X^\top X + \lambda I)^{-1}X^\top\mathbf{y}\) show about how ridge handles collinearity or the \(p>n\) case?

Hint

What does adding \(\lambda I\) do to the eigenvalues of \(X^\top X\)?

Answer

Adding \(\lambda I\) raises every eigenvalue of \(X^\top X\) by \(\lambda\), so a zero (or near-zero) eigenvalue — which makes \(X^\top X\) singular / non-invertible — becomes \(\lambda > 0\). The matrix is then invertible and well-conditioned, giving stable coefficients even when predictors are collinear or \(p > n\).

Q6. What is the elastic net, and what practical problem with lasso does it fix in genomics?

Hint

It mixes two penalties with a parameter \(\alpha\). Think about a group of co-regulated genes.

Answer

Elastic net uses a mix of L1 and L2: penalty \(= \alpha\sum_j\lvert\beta_j\rvert + (1-\alpha)\sum_j\beta_j^2\), with \(\alpha=1\) pure lasso and \(\alpha=0\) pure ridge. It fixes lasso’s tendency to pick one gene arbitrarily from a group of correlated (co-regulated) genes and drop the rest: the L2 part encourages correlated genes to be selected or dropped together (the grouping effect), giving more stable, biologically sensible selection.

Q7. How should you choose \(\lambda\), and why can you not just pick the \(\lambda\) that minimizes the training error?

Hint

What happens to training error as \(\lambda \to 0\)? Which model does that favour?

Answer

Choose \(\lambda\) by cross-validation — estimate error on held-out folds and pick the \(\lambda\) with the lowest cross-validated error (lambda.min, or the sparser lambda.1se within one SE). You cannot use training error because it falls monotonically as \(\lambda \to 0\), so it would always select the overfit OLS model. Only out-of-sample error reveals the right amount of shrinkage.

Q8. You run cv.glmnet(x, y, alpha = 1) and extract coefficients at lambda.1se. Most entries are zero; a few genes are nonzero. In biological terms, what is that short list, and what is one important caveat about it?

Hint

Selected genes are a signature. Does “selected” mean “causal”?

Answer

The nonzero genes are the model’s selected predictive signature — a short, testable panel that predicts the phenotype and could be turned into a targeted assay. Caveat: these genes are predictive, not proven causal. Because of collinearity a dropped gene may be just as relevant as the one kept, and the exact list can shift across CV splits at small \(n\). Interpret it as a hypothesis to validate experimentally, not as established biology.
