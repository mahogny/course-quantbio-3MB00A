Exercise: penalized models — case analysis & pitfalls

Goal

By the end of this exercise you should be able to:

  • explain why correlated predictors make lasso selection unstable (identifiability), and show the mechanism on a tiny by-hand example,
  • contrast how lasso, ridge, and elastic net treat a correlated group,
  • carry out stability selection — resample, refit, and turn the results into a selection frequency — both by hand and in R,
  • read a selection-frequency result and decide what is a defensible signature,
  • interpret a penalized fit honestly: distinguish predictive from causal, and know why naive post-selection p-values are invalid,
  • write a reproducible methods paragraph for a penalized analysis.

This is a judgement unit. The by-hand parts build the intuition the exam can probe; the R parts are the workflow you would actually run. Full R fluency with glmnet came in Ridge, lasso & elastic net and Cross-validation & model selection — here we reuse it and focus on the pitfalls.

Note

Provisional block. Penalized linear models may move to a later statistics / machine-learning course; treat this unit as a first pass.

Background

We fit a penalized linear model to a samples × features matrix \(X\) and a response \(y\). For the lasso,

\[\hat{\boldsymbol\beta} = \arg\min_{\boldsymbol\beta}\ \tfrac{1}{2n}\sum_{i=1}^n \big(y_i - \beta_0 - \mathbf{x}_i^\top\boldsymbol\beta\big)^2 \;+\; \lambda \sum_{j=1}^p |\beta_j|,\]

with \(\lambda\) chosen by cross-validation. The penalty is scale-dependent, so predictors are standardized first (glmnet does this internally).

The recurring problem. Biological features are correlated — genes in a pathway or co-expression module rise and fall together, so \(X\) has blocks of near-duplicate columns. When two columns are near-identical:

  • the lasso puts essentially all the weight on one of them and zeros the rest; which one it picks is decided by a sliver of sample-specific noise, so it flips under tiny data changes,
  • the ridge penalty (\(\sum \beta_j^2\)) instead spreads the weight across the group, and
  • the elastic net (\(0<\alpha<1\)) keeps correlated members together (the grouping effect).

Stability selection tames the instability: subsample the data many times, refit the lasso each time, and record each feature’s selection frequency \(\pi_j\) = fraction of subsamples in which it was chosen. Keep features above a stated threshold.

Two interpretation rules to carry throughout:

  • Selected ≠ causal. A chosen feature may be a proxy, a downstream consequence, or a confounder marker. A penalized fit yields hypotheses, not mechanisms.
  • Coefficients are shrunk (biased toward zero) and you may not compute naive p-values for selected features on the same data (post-selection inference).

By hand

B1. (identifiability — the core mechanism). Two features are identical across three samples: column \(x_1 = x_2 = (1, 2, 3)^\top\). Suppose the fit wants the combined contribution \(\beta_1 x_1 + \beta_2 x_2\) to equal \(2\,x_1\) — i.e. any split with \(\beta_1 + \beta_2 = 2\) (and both \(\ge 0\)) fits the data equally well. Consider three splits: \((\beta_1,\beta_2) = (2,0)\), \((1,1)\), \((0,2)\). (a) Compute the lasso penalty \(|\beta_1| + |\beta_2|\) for each split. Which split does the lasso prefer? (b) Compute the ridge penalty \(\beta_1^2 + \beta_2^2\) for each split. Which split does the ridge prefer? (c) Explain, from (a), why the lasso’s choice among these features is unstable, and from (b), why the ridge spreads weight across the group.

B2. (which one does the lasso pick). Now make the two features nearly identical: \(x_2\) is \(x_1\) with a tiny perturbation, and \(x_1\) happens to correlate very slightly more strongly with \(y\) than \(x_2\) does. Argue in one or two sentences which feature the lasso keeps, and what would happen to that choice if you dropped one sample and \(x_2\) became the marginally stronger one.

B3. (stability selection by hand). You run the lasso on 5 random subsamples of your data. The table marks (×) which of five genes was selected in each subsample:

subsample GENE_A GENE_B GENE_C GENE_D GENE_E
1 × × ×
2 × × ×
3 × × ×
4 × × ×
5 × × ×
  1. Compute each gene’s selection frequency \(\pi_j\) (out of 5).
  2. With a threshold of \(\pi \ge 0.6\), which genes make the shortlist?
  3. GENE_C and GENE_D are known to be co-expressed. Their frequencies are moderate. What is the biologically honest way to report them?

B4. (selected ≠ causal). For each finding, name the most likely reason a predictive gene need not be causal — proxy (correlated non-functional), reverse causation (consequence of the phenotype), or confounder marker (tracks batch/purity): (a) The gene is a well-known marker of tumour purity, and drug response also depends on purity. (b) The gene is strongly co-expressed with the pathway’s true driver but has no known function of its own. (c) The phenotype (rapid proliferation) is what drives up this gene’s expression, not the other way round.

B5. (reporting). A methods paragraph reads: “We fit a lasso with glmnet and report the selected genes as the drug-response signature.” List three pieces of information a reader needs before they can reproduce or trust this, and name one claim the sentence risks over-stating.

Explore: which gene does the lasso keep?

The demo shows B1–B2 for two standardized genes. The grey ellipses are RSS contours around the OLS point (drag it), the green region is the penalty region at the current \(\lambda\), and the purple dot is the penalized estimate where the smallest contour touches the region. “corr” is the correlation between the two genes; at \(0.9\) the ellipses become a long valley along which many splits of the weight fit almost equally well. The right panel shows the coefficient path against \(\lambda\). (The demo uses the objective \(\lVert\mathbf{y}-X\boldsymbol\beta\rVert^2 + \lambda\,\text{penalty}\), so its \(\lambda\) is on a different scale from the \(\tfrac{1}{2n}\) version above.)

E1. After Reset (lasso, corr \(0.9\), OLS \(= (1.5, 0.5)\), \(\lambda = 2.5\)), which gene does the lasso keep? Drag the OLS point along the valley to about \((0.5, 1.5)\). Which gene is kept now? Relate this to B2.

E2. Repeat E1 with Ridge and with Elastic net (\(\alpha = 0.5\)). How are the two coefficients split at the two OLS positions?

E3. Back to the lasso at OLS \((1.5, 0.5)\): lower \(\lambda\) to \(0.5\). How many genes are in the model? Use the path panel to read off roughly the \(\lambda\) at which gene 2 drops out.

Solutions

E1. At OLS \((1.5, 0.5)\) the lasso gives \(\hat{\boldsymbol\beta} \approx (0.70, 0)\): gene 1 is kept and gene 2 is exactly zero (the solution is on a corner of the diamond). At \((0.5, 1.5)\) it gives \(\approx (0, 0.70)\): the choice flips to gene 2. With strongly correlated genes the OLS point is poorly determined along the valley, so a small change in the data (one sample dropped) can move it from one side of the diagonal to the other, and the lasso’s pick flips with it, as argued in B2.

E2. Ridge gives about \((0.45, 0.41)\) and \((0.41, 0.45)\); elastic net about \((0.44, 0.37)\) and \((0.37, 0.44)\). Both keep both genes with similar weights, and moving the OLS point only shifts the weights a little: the weight is spread across the group (ridge, B1(b)) and correlated genes stay in together (the elastic-net grouping effect).

E3. At \(\lambda = 0.5\) both genes are in the model, about \((1.37, 0.37)\). On the path, \(\hat\beta_2\) reaches zero at about \(\lambda \approx 1.9\), and \(\hat\beta_1\) at about \(\lambda \approx 3.9\). Which gene survives longest is decided by which side of the valley the OLS point fell on.

In R

Use these as static references — read them, predict the output, then run them in your own session. Assume x is a standardized samples × genes matrix and y a numeric response.

Fit the lasso and read the sparse signature

library(glmnet)

fit <- cv.glmnet(x, y, alpha = 1, standardize = TRUE, nfolds = 10)
sel <- function(cvfit) which(as.vector(coef(cvfit, s = "lambda.1se"))[-1] != 0)
colnames(x)[sel(fit)]      # the (unstable) one-shot shortlist

See the instability: refit after a tiny change

set.seed(1)
fit_all  <- cv.glmnet(x,       y,      alpha = 1)
fit_drop <- cv.glmnet(x[-1, ], y[-1],  alpha = 1)   # drop one sample

setdiff(colnames(x)[sel(fit_all)],  colnames(x)[sel(fit_drop)])  # genes lost
setdiff(colnames(x)[sel(fit_drop)], colnames(x)[sel(fit_all)])   # genes gained

Lasso vs ridge on a correlated pair

# a and b are near-duplicate columns; compare how each penalty splits the weight
las   <- glmnet(x, y, alpha = 1, lambda = 0.05)
rdg   <- glmnet(x, y, alpha = 0, lambda = 0.05)
coef(las)[c("GENE_a", "GENE_b"), ]   # lasso: weight piles on one, other ~ 0
coef(rdg)[c("GENE_a", "GENE_b"), ]   # ridge: weight split across both

Stability selection

set.seed(1)
B <- 100
n <- nrow(x); p <- ncol(x)
counts <- integer(p)

for (b in seq_len(B)) {
  idx    <- sample(n, floor(n / 2))            # a random half of the samples
  fb     <- cv.glmnet(x[idx, ], y[idx], alpha = 1)
  beta   <- as.vector(coef(fb, s = "lambda.1se"))[-1]   # drop intercept
  counts <- counts + as.integer(beta != 0)
}

freq        <- counts / B
names(freq) <- colnames(x)
stable      <- sort(freq[freq >= 0.6], decreasing = TRUE)
stable

Read the frequency profile

barplot(sort(freq, decreasing = TRUE)[1:30],
        las = 2, ylab = "selection frequency")
abline(h = 0.6, col = "red", lty = 2)         # your reporting threshold

Tasks

R1. Run the instability block with three different seeds (set.seed(1), set.seed(2), set.seed(3)), each time comparing the one-shot lasso shortlist across the full data vs one dropped sample. Do the gained/lost genes tend to be co-expressed with the ones they replace? (Inspect a small gene–gene correlation on the affected genes.)

R2. Compare the lasso and ridge coefficients for a known correlated gene pair (the “lasso vs ridge” block). Confirm that the lasso concentrates the weight on one member while the ridge spreads it. Then fit an elastic net (alpha = 0.5) and describe where it sits between the two.

R3. Run stability selection with B = 100. Produce the sorted selection-frequency bar plot with the threshold line. Is there a clear gap between a stable head and a noisy tail, or is the distribution smeared? State, in one sentence, what your plot licenses you to report.

R4. Take your stable shortlist from R3 and, for the top gene, write one sentence that reports it correctly — i.e. as a stably selected predictor and a hypothesis, not as a causal effect with a p-value.

Hints

Hint 1 — B1, comparing the two penalties

For identical columns, every split with \(\beta_1 + \beta_2 = 2\) (both \(\ge 0\)) gives the same fitted values, so only the penalty decides. The lasso penalty is \(|\beta_1| + |\beta_2|\); the ridge penalty is \(\beta_1^2 + \beta_2^2\). Plug in \((2,0)\), \((1,1)\), \((0,2)\) and compare. One penalty gives a tie; the other has a unique minimum.

Hint 2 — B1(c), why a tie means instability

If several solutions are tied, the choice among them is decided by whatever breaks the tie — here, tiny sample-specific noise. A method with a unique minimum (ridge) is stable; a method with a tie (lasso on identical columns) is not. See Ridge, lasso & elastic net for the diamond-vs-circle geometry behind this.

Hint 3 — B3, selection frequency

Selection frequency for a gene is simply (number of subsamples in which it was selected) / (total subsamples). Count the × marks down each column and divide by 5. A threshold of \(\pi \ge 0.6\) means “selected in at least 3 of the 5 subsamples”.

Hint 4 — B3(c), reporting a split module

When two co-expressed genes each sit at a moderate frequency, the resamples are splitting their votes between interchangeable members. The stable finding is not “gene C” or “gene D” but the module they belong to.

Hint 5 — B4, three ways to be non-causal

Proxy: rides on a correlated true driver, no function of its own. Reverse causation: the phenotype changes the gene, not vice versa. Confounder marker: tracks a third variable (batch, tumour purity) that independently drives the response. Match each scenario to the phrase that fits.

Hint 6 — B5, what a methods paragraph must state

Think reproducibility: standardization, how \(\lambda\) (and \(\alpha\)) were chosen and by what CV, whether stability selection was used and with what \(B\) and threshold, and the random seed. The over-statement to watch for is treating a selected gene as a causal one. See the reporting slide in the lecture.

Hint 7 — the R workflow

Build the matrix with as.matrix; fit with cv.glmnet (alpha = 1 lasso, 0 ridge, between = elastic net); pull coefficients at s = "lambda.1se" with coef. Resample rows with sample and always set.seed so results are reproducible. colMeans of a 0/1 selection matrix (or counts / B) gives the selection frequency.

Solutions

Solution

B1. (a) Lasso penalty \(|\beta_1| + |\beta_2|\): \((2,0) \to 2+0 = 2\); \((1,1) \to 1+1 = 2\); \((0,2) \to 0+2 = 2\). All equal 2 — the lasso is indifferent among these splits (a tie). It does not prefer any one; the solution is non-unique. (b) Ridge penalty \(\beta_1^2 + \beta_2^2\): \((2,0) \to 4+0 = 4\); \((1,1) \to 1+1 = 2\); \((0,2) \to 0+4 = 4\). The even split \((1,1)\) is the unique minimum (2 < 4) — the ridge spreads the weight across the group. (c) Because the lasso penalty is tied across all same-sign splits summing to 2, the choice among the interchangeable features is decided by whatever tiny difference breaks the tie — i.e. sample-specific noise. Perturb the data slightly and the “winning” feature flips: the selection is unstable. The ridge, having a strict unique minimum at the even split, is stable and keeps both features (but never gives you an exact zero, so it is not sparse).

B2. The lasso keeps the feature with the marginally stronger correlation with \(y\) — here \(x_1\) — and drives \(x_2\) to zero, because concentrating the weight on the slightly-better predictor gives a marginally lower loss for the same penalty. This margin is tiny and noise-driven: if dropping one sample makes \(x_2\) the marginally stronger predictor, the lasso will switch to keeping \(x_2\) and zero \(x_1\). The two are near-interchangeable, so the predictions barely change while the selected gene flips — the identifiability problem in action.

B3. (a) Count × down each column, divide by 5: GENE_A: 5/5 = 1.0; GENE_B: 3/5 = 0.6; GENE_C: 2/5 = 0.4; GENE_D: 3/5 = 0.6; GENE_E: 1/5 = 0.2. (b) Threshold \(\pi \ge 0.6\): GENE_A (1.0), GENE_B (0.6), GENE_D (0.6) make the shortlist. GENE_C (0.4) and GENE_E (0.2) do not. (c) GENE_C and GENE_D are co-expressed and each has only moderate frequency (0.4 and 0.6) — the resamples are splitting their votes between two interchangeable members. The honest report is that the co-expression module (containing C and D) is associated with the response, rather than singling out D and discarding C as if they were independent findings. (Note GENE_D clears the threshold and C does not, but their difference of one subsample is within resampling noise.)

B4. (a) Confounder marker — the gene tracks tumour purity, and purity independently drives drug response, so the gene predicts \(y\) without acting on it. (b) Proxy — it is correlated with the true driver but has no function of its own; it predicts by association. (c) Reverse causation — the phenotype (proliferation) drives the gene up, so the causal arrow points from response to gene, not gene to response.

B5. Three things a reader needs (any three): (i) that predictors were standardized (and any supervised preprocessing was inside the CV, not before it); (ii) how \(\lambda\) — and \(\alpha\) if elastic net — was chosen and by what cross-validation scheme (e.g. 10-fold, lambda.1se); (iii) whether stability selection was used, with what number of resamples \(B\) and what threshold, and the random seed for reproducibility. Also acceptable: held-out performance on data untouched by tuning. The over-statement: calling the selected genes “the signature” implies they are the important / causal genes, when a lasso selection is an unstable, predictive list — a set of hypotheses, not established drivers.

R1. Across seeds you should see the one-shot shortlist wobble: genes drop out and near-duplicates take their place. Inspecting the correlation of a lost gene against its replacement typically shows a high correlation — they are members of the same co-expression module, so the lasso is swapping interchangeable columns. Example skeleton:

sel <- function(cvfit) which(as.vector(coef(cvfit, s = "lambda.1se"))[-1] != 0)

for (s in 1:3) {
  set.seed(s)
  fa <- cv.glmnet(x,       y,     alpha = 1)
  fd <- cv.glmnet(x[-1, ], y[-1], alpha = 1)
  cat("seed", s,
      "| lost:",   setdiff(colnames(x)[sel(fa)], colnames(x)[sel(fd)]),
      "| gained:", setdiff(colnames(x)[sel(fd)], colnames(x)[sel(fa)]), "\n")
}
# then, for a lost/gained pair g1, g2:
cor(x[, "g1"], x[, "g2"])     # typically high -> same module

R2. The lasso puts (almost) all the weight on one member of the correlated pair and leaves the other near zero; the ridge gives both a similar, smaller coefficient (weight spread). The elastic net (alpha = 0.5) sits between: it keeps both members (like ridge’s grouping) but shrinks small ones toward zero (like the lasso), so a correlated pair tends to enter or leave together with modest coefficients.

las  <- glmnet(x, y, alpha = 1,   lambda = 0.05)
rdg  <- glmnet(x, y, alpha = 0,   lambda = 0.05)
enet <- glmnet(x, y, alpha = 0.5, lambda = 0.05)
coef(las )[c("GENE_a", "GENE_b"), ]   # one big, one ~0
coef(rdg )[c("GENE_a", "GENE_b"), ]   # two similar, smaller
coef(enet)[c("GENE_a", "GENE_b"), ]   # both kept, shrunk

R3. Running the stability-selection loop and plotting the sorted frequencies, a clear gap between a handful of high-frequency genes and a long low tail means the data support a defensible signature (report the genes above the threshold). A smeared distribution with everything around 0.3–0.5 means no gene is reliably selected — the honest conclusion is that the data cannot support a stable signature at this sample size, and you should say so rather than lower the threshold until a story appears. What the plot licenses you to report is exactly the set of genes clearing the stated threshold, plus any module whose members split moderate votes.

R4. A correct one-sentence report, e.g.: “TYMS was selected in 94% of subsampled lasso fits, marking it as a stable transcriptomic predictor of drug response and a candidate for functional validation” — note it reports a selection frequency and frames the gene as a predictive hypothesis to be tested, with no effect-size p-value and no causal claim.

Where this leads

  • Cross-validation & model selection: the \(\lambda\) (and \(\alpha\)) tuning used here relies on the CV machinery from that unit; remember that the CV used to tune cannot also certify performance — that needs a held-out set or nested CV.
  • Penalties as Bayesian priors: the lasso penalty is a Laplace prior and the ridge penalty a Gaussian prior (the MAP view). Shrinkage bias is the prior pulling estimates toward zero — which is why coefficients are not unbiased effect sizes.
  • Beyond this course: valid inference after selection (selective / post-selection inference) and formal stability-selection error control are topics for a later statistics / machine-learning course, in line with this block’s provisional status.
