Quiz: cell-type annotation

Short questions to test your understanding of annotating single-cell clusters. Try each on paper first, then open the answer. Several are written in the style of the on-campus exam (no computer).

Q1. What is the difference between a cluster and a cell type?

Hint

One is produced by an algorithm; the other is a biological label.

Answer

A cluster is a group of similar cells found by an unsupervised algorithm — it is just an anonymous label (“cluster 3”). A cell type is the biological identity (T cell, macrophage). Annotation is the step that attaches a cell-type name to a cluster, using marker genes or a reference.

Q2. In a one-vs-rest marker search, a gene has avg_log2FC = 3. How many-fold higher is its mean expression in the cluster than in the rest?

Hint

The value is a \(\log_2\); invert it.

Answer

\(2^3 = 8\)-fold higher. avg_log2FC is the \(\log_2\) of the ratio of mean expression (cluster / rest), so exponentiate base 2 to get the fold change.

Q3. In a FindMarkers table, a gene for cluster 3 shows pct.1 = 0.95 and pct.2 = 0.08. What do these two numbers tell you, and is this a good marker?

Hint

pct.1 is inside the cluster, pct.2 is outside. Think sensitivity vs specificity.

Answer

pct.1 = 0.95: the gene is detected in 95% of the cluster cells (sensitive — on in almost all of them). pct.2 = 0.08: detected in only 8% of the rest (specific — off elsewhere). High pct.1 with low pct.2 is exactly the profile of a good marker.

Q4. Why is the default differential-expression test for markers the Wilcoxon rank-sum test rather than a t-test?

Hint

Think about the distribution of single-cell counts — are they Gaussian?

Answer

Single-cell expression is sparse, skewed, and overdispersed (count data, not Gaussian — recall the negative-binomial story). The Wilcoxon rank-sum test is non-parametric: it only asks whether one group ranks systematically higher, so it does not assume normality and is robust to the many zeros and the long tail.

Q5. A cluster’s p_val_adj for its top gene is essentially \(0\), yet you are told a tiny p-value alone does not confirm a marker. Why not?

Hint

How many cells are in the test, and what does that do to p-values?

Answer

With thousands of cells per group, even a biologically trivial difference becomes statistically significant, so almost every gene gets a tiny p-value. Significance must be paired with effect size (avg_log2FC) and detection (pct.1 high, pct.2 low). The p-value says the difference is real; the fold change and pct columns say whether it is useful as a marker. (The p-value is also adjusted for multiple testing because ~20 000 genes are tested per cluster.)

Q6. You find a small cluster that expresses both CD3D (a T-cell marker) and MS4A1 (a B-cell marker) at high level. What is the most likely explanation, and what should you do?

Hint

Two exclusive lineages in “one cell.” What could put two cells’ RNA together?

Answer

Most likely a doublet cluster — droplets that captured two cells (a T cell and a B cell), whose profile is the sum of both types. Do not call a new “T/B hybrid” type. Run a doublet detector (scDblFinder, DoubletFinder), remove flagged cells, and re-check. Co-expression of two lineages’ exclusive markers is a doublet until proven otherwise.

Q7. Contrast manual (marker-based) and reference-based annotation. Give one strength of each and the main weakness of reference-based annotation.

Hint

Where does each get its knowledge — a curated marker list, or a labelled dataset?

Answer

Manual: match a cluster’s markers to curated signatures — transparent and encodes expert knowledge, good for novel or unusual tissue, but slow and subjective. Reference-based (e.g. SingleR, Azimuth, CellTypist): copy labels from a labelled atlas by similarity (correlation) to reference profiles — fast, consistent, per-cell. Its main weakness: it is only as good as the reference — a cell type absent from the reference cannot be labelled correctly and will be forced onto the nearest wrong label. In practice the two are combined.

Q8. In a developing tissue, cells lie on a smooth gradient from progenitor to mature. What is the danger of forcing them into discrete cluster labels, and what alternative better matches the biology?

Hint

Is identity here a set of boxes or a continuum?

Answer

Clustering discretises what is really a continuum, so the boundaries — and therefore the “types” — are partly arbitrary and can be artefacts of where the algorithm cut the gradient. A trajectory / pseudotime analysis, which orders cells along the continuous developmental axis instead of boxing them, is truer to the biology. (Activation states and the cell cycle are other continuous states that resist discrete labels.)
