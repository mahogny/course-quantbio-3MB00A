Exercise: error models — exponential & Poisson

Goal

By the end of this exercise you should be able to:

  • explain what an error model (likelihood) is and why every analysis assumes one,
  • match a data type to the right distribution — Gaussian, Poisson, exponential, negative binomial,
  • use the mean = variance identity of the Poisson by hand and as a diagnostic,
  • read Poisson probabilities off a simulation or dpois, and use mean gap = 1 / rate for waiting times,
  • explain overdispersion and why RNA-seq counts need the negative binomial,
  • explain, concretely, why the wrong error model misleads (inflated false positives).

In Bulk RNA-seq with DESeq2 you saw that ratios of small counts are noisy (about \(n \pm \sqrt{n}\), the MA-plot fan). Here that noise gets a name: the Poisson is the counting noise, and the negative binomial adds the biological spread between replicates.

This is a concept day. Spend most of your time reasoning about which distribution fits and why; the demos and the R section are there to confirm your answers and build intuition by simulation. No formula needs to be evaluated by hand; items marked (optional) are for those who want the maths.

Background

A measurement is signal plus noise. The error model is the probability distribution you assume the data came from given the truth; read as a function of the parameters, it is the likelihood (the same likelihood that multiplied the prior in Bayesian vs frequentist).

The four distributions of this unit:

Data type Distribution Mean Variance
Continuous, symmetric Gaussian \(\mathcal{N}(\mu,\sigma^2)\) \(\mu\) \(\sigma^2\) (independent of \(\mu\))
Counts (idealised) Poisson\((\lambda)\) \(\lambda\) \(\lambda\)
Waiting times / gaps Exponential\((\lambda)\) \(1/\lambda\) \(1/\lambda^2\)
Overdispersed counts Negative binomial\((\mu,\phi)\) \(\mu\) \(\mu + \phi\mu^2\)
Optional: the maths

\[P_{\text{Pois}}(X=k) = \frac{\lambda^k e^{-\lambda}}{k!}, \qquad f_{\text{Exp}}(t) = \lambda e^{-\lambda t}, \qquad P_{\text{Exp}}(T > t) = e^{-\lambda t}.\]

R computes these for you: dpois(k, lambda) and pexp(t, lambda, lower.tail = FALSE).

Poisson process: one stream of random events at rate \(\lambda\). The number in a fixed window is Poisson\((\lambda)\); the waiting time to the next event is Exponential\((\lambda)\).

Overdispersion: real biological counts have variance > mean, because the true rate varies between replicates. The negative binomial adds a dispersion parameter to absorb this (\(\phi = 0\) recovers Poisson).

By hand

B1. For each dataset, name the error model you would use and give the one-line reason: (a) cell diameters in micrometres; (b) reads mapped to a gene across 5 technical replicates; (c) time between neuronal spikes; (d) reads per gene across 5 biological replicates (different mice).

B2. A gene has an average of \(\lambda = 2\) reads per sample under a Poisson model. R’s dpois(0:4, 2) gives the probabilities of 0, 1, 2, 3 and 4 reads:

reads 0 1 2 3 4
probability 0.135 0.271 0.271 0.180 0.090
  1. What are \(P(X=0)\), \(P(X=1)\) and \(P(X=2)\)? Which counts are most likely?
  2. What is \(P(X \ge 1)\), the chance of seeing the gene at all? (Use the complement.)
  3. In about what fraction of samples would you see 2 reads or fewer?
  4. (optional) Check \(P(X=0)\), \(P(X=1)\) and \(P(X=2)\) by hand from the formula in the optional maths box above.

B3. For a Poisson\((\lambda)\), state the mean and the variance. A colleague reports count data with sample mean \(\approx 20\) and sample variance \(\approx 210\). Is Poisson an adequate error model? What does the discrepancy tell you, and which distribution should they use instead?

B4. Mutations occur along a genome at rate \(\lambda = 0.5\) per megabase, modelled as a Poisson process. (a) What is the expected waiting distance to the next mutation? (b) The number of mutations in a 2-Mb window is Poisson with mean \(0.5 \times 2 = 1\), and dpois(0, 1) gives \(0.37\). What is \(P(\text{no mutation in the next 2 Mb})\)? Explain in words why this is the same as “the gap to the next mutation is longer than 2 Mb”.

B5. A negative binomial has \(\text{Var}(X) = \mu + \phi\mu^2\). For \(\mu = 50\): (a) what is the variance when \(\phi = 0\), and what distribution is that? (b) what is the variance when \(\phi = 0.5\)? (c) A Poisson-based test analyses these \(\phi = 0.5\) data. Will its p-values be too large or too small, and does that inflate false positives or false negatives?

B6. True or false, with one line of reasoning each: (a) “A Gaussian is fine for counts as long as the mean is large.” (b) “If counts are Poisson, plotting sample variance against sample mean across many genes should fall near the line \(y = x\).” (c) “The exponential distribution can produce negative waiting times.”

B7. (waiting-time flavour) A DNA-binding protein has a dwell time on its site that is exponential with mean \(4\) seconds. (a) What is the rate \(\lambda\) (unbindings per second)? (b) One protein has just bound; another has already stayed 4 s. Which of the two is more likely to still be bound 4 s from now — or are they equally likely? Which property of the exponential is this? (E6 lets you see it.) (c) (optional) Compute \(P(\text{dwell} > 4\text{ s})\), and the probability that a protein that has already stayed 4 s stays at least 4 s more.

Explore

Poisson vs negative binomial

Do B3 and B5 by hand first. The demo draws 1000 counts from a negative binomial with mean \(\mu\) and dispersion \(\phi\), in this unit’s convention \(\text{Var}(X) = \mu + \phi\mu^2\). The bars are the simulated counts, the red dots the NB probabilities, and the dashed blue outline the Poisson with the same mean.

E1. At \(\mu = 50\), read the theoretical variance for \(\phi = 0.5\), then click Poisson. Do the numbers match your B5 answers? What happens to the histogram and the dashed outline when \(\phi = 0\)?

E2. Back at \(\phi = 0.5\), click draw again a few times. Compare the sample mean and sample variance with theory. Which of the two jumps around more?

E3. Click low mean (\(\mu = 3\)), then step through Poisson, mild overdispersion and strong (many zeros). How many of the 1000 cells have a zero count in each case? What happens to the mean?

E4. Set \(\phi = 0.1\) and move \(\mu\) through 5, 50 and 500. Read var/mean each time (tick mean–variance plot to see it on log axes). Then find the \(\phi\) whose variance at \(\mu = 20\) is closest to the colleague’s 210 in B3.

Solutions

E1. \(\phi = 0.5\): \(50 + 0.5\cdot 50^2 = 1300\). \(\phi = 0\): variance \(= 50\), var/mean \(= 1\): the Poisson (B5a–b). At \(\phi = 0\) the histogram falls on the dashed Poisson outline.

E2. The first draw gives a sample mean of 50.0 and a sample variance of about 1390 (theory 50 and 1300); further draws (about 1280, 1200, 1250) move by a hundred or more, while the sample means stay within about 1 of 50. Variances are harder to estimate than means, especially for overdispersed counts, which is why tools like DESeq2 share dispersion information across genes.

E3. With \(\mu = 3\) (first draw): Poisson gives 49 zeros (theory \(1000\,e^{-3} \approx 50\)); mild overdispersion (\(\phi = 0.1\)) 69; strong (\(\phi = 2\)) 368, more than a third of all cells (theory \(1000\,(1+\phi\mu)^{-1/\phi} \approx 378\)). The theoretical mean stays at 3 throughout (the sample means stay close to it): the NB moves probability both to zero and to a long right tail.

E4. With \(\phi = 0.1\): var/mean \(= 1 + \phi\mu\) is 1.5 at \(\mu = 5\), 6 at \(\mu = 50\) and 51 at \(\mu = 500\); on log axes the NB curve peels away from the line var \(= \mu\) as \(\mu\) grows. At \(\mu = 20\), \(210 = 20 + 400\,\phi\) gives \(\phi \approx 0.48\); the slider’s \(\phi = 0.5\) (variance 220) is closest, so the data in B3 are clearly overdispersed.

Counts and gaps: one process

Do B4 first. The demo scatters random events (mutations) along a 2000-Mb genome at a given rate; the strip shows the first 10 Mb. Left: how many events fall in each window (bars) against the Poisson (red dots). Right: the gaps between neighbouring events against the exponential (red curve).

E5. At the start (2 per Mb, 1-Mb windows), read the mean and variance of the counts and the mean gap. Which identity does each illustrate? Then set the rate to 0.5 and the window to 2 Mb, as in B4. What fraction of windows have no mutation, and what is the mean gap? Compare with your B4 answers.

E6. Still at rate 0.5, tick memoryless and set already waited to 2. The demo keeps only the gaps longer than 2 Mb and shows how much of each is left beyond 2. What fraction of gaps are longer than 2 Mb, and what is the mean of what is left? What does that say about B7(b)? Finally tick clumped (events in bursts): what happens to the variance of the counts and to the wait left?

Solutions

E5. At rate 2: 4077 mutations, counts with mean 2.04 and variance 2.00 (mean = variance, the Poisson signature); mean gap 0.49 Mb \(\approx\) 1 / rate = 0.5. At rate 0.5 with 2-Mb windows (first draw): 998 mutations in 1000 windows, mean 1.00, variance 0.93; 36.2% of windows are empty (B4b: 0.37), and the mean gap is 2.00 Mb (B4a: 1 / 0.5 = 2).

E6. In the first draw, 37.6% of the gaps are longer than 2 Mb (again about 0.37, B4b seen from the gap side), and what is left of them has mean 1.95 Mb, the same as all gaps (2.00). Having gone 2 Mb without a mutation does not bring the next one closer: the exponential is memoryless. For B7(b) this means the protein that has already stayed 4 s is just as likely to stay another 4 s as the freshly bound one. With clumped on, the counts have mean 1.04 but variance 3.38 (overdispersed, about 3 times the mean), and the wait left after 2 Mb is 5.78 Mb, far longer than the mean gap: a bursty process remembers, so neither the Poisson nor the exponential fits.

In R

Use R to confirm your hand answers and to see the distributions. These are static illustrations; run them in your own session.

Poisson probabilities and the mean = variance identity

lambda <- 2
dpois(0:3, lambda)          # P(X=0..3);  P(0)=e^-2 ~ 0.135
1 - dpois(0, lambda)        # P(X>=1) = 1 - P(0)

x <- rpois(1e5, lambda)     # simulate
mean(x); var(x)             # both ~ 2  ->  mean = variance

Exponential waiting times

lambda <- 0.5               # events per Mb
pexp(2, rate = lambda, lower.tail = FALSE)  # P(T > 2) = e^{-1} ~ 0.368
mean(rexp(1e5, rate = lambda))  # ~ 2 = 1/lambda

# same process, counted per 2-Mb window: Poisson(lambda * 2) = Poisson(1)
dpois(0, lambda * 2)        # P(0 events in 2 Mb) = e^{-1}  -> matches

Poisson vs negative binomial: same mean, different spread

mu <- 50
mean(rpois(1e5, mu));            var(rpois(1e5, mu))            # ~50, ~50
phi <- 0.5                       # Var = mu + phi * mu^2 = 50 + 1250 = 1300
y <- rnbinom(1e5, mu = mu, size = 1 / phi)   # R's knob is called size = 1/phi
mean(y); var(y)                  # ~50, ~1300 >> 50

Tasks

R1. Run dpois(0:4, 2) to reproduce the table in B2, and check 1 - dpois(0, 2) against your \(P(X \ge 1)\) and ppois(2, 2) against your answer to B2(c).

R2. For B3, simulate x <- rpois(1e5, 20) and report mean(x) and var(x). Then simulate overdispersed counts with dispersion \(\phi = 0.25\), rnbinom(1e5, mu = 20, size = 1 / 0.25), and report its variance. Which one resembles the colleague’s data (mean \(\approx 20\), variance \(\approx 210\))? (Recall \(\text{Var} = \mu + \phi\mu^2\).)

R3. For B4, verify both routes agree: pexp(2, rate = 0.5, lower.tail = FALSE) (the chance that the gap is longer than 2 Mb) and dpois(0, 0.5 * 2) (the chance of zero mutations in a 2-Mb window). They should give the same number.

Hints

Hint 1 — picking the distribution (B1, B6)

Ask two questions in order. First: continuous or a count? Continuous symmetric measurements \(\to\) Gaussian; continuous non-negative waiting times \(\to\) exponential; counts \(\to\) Poisson or negative binomial. Second, for counts: technical/idealised or biological replicates? Biological replicates are almost always overdispersed (variance > mean) \(\to\) negative binomial.

Hint 2 — Poisson probabilities (B2)

Read the probabilities straight off the table. For “at least one”, use the complement: \(P(X \ge 1) = 1 - P(X = 0)\). For “2 or fewer”, add the bars for 0, 1 and 2. (Optional, by hand: \(P(X=k) = \lambda^k e^{-\lambda}/k!\) with \(\lambda = 2\); note \(0! = 1! = 1\) and \(2! = 2\).) See dpois.

Hint 3 — the mean = variance diagnostic (B3)

Poisson forces \(\text{Var} = \text{mean}\). If the sample variance is much bigger than the sample mean, the data are overdispersed and Poisson understates the noise. The fix is the negative binomial, whose variance \(\mu + \phi\mu^2\) can exceed the mean.

Hint 4 — exponential vs Poisson for the same process (B4)

For a Poisson process at rate \(\lambda\): the mean gap to the next event is \(1/\lambda\), and the count in a window of length \(t\) is Poisson with mean \(\lambda t\). “No mutation in the next 2 Mb” and “the next mutation is more than 2 Mb away” describe the same stretch of genome, so they must have the same probability. See pexp and dpois.

Hint 5 — negative binomial variance (B5)

Plug into \(\text{Var} = \mu + \phi\mu^2\) (in R, pass size = 1/phi). A test that assumes less variance than the truth makes p-values too small (overconfident), which produces false positives. See rnbinom.

Hint 6 — memorylessness (B7)

Mean of an exponential is \(1/\lambda\), so \(\lambda = 1/\text{mean}\). For (b): in E6, compare the leftover waits of the gaps that are already long with all gaps. (Optional, for (c): \(P(T > t) = e^{-\lambda t}\), and memorylessness means \(P(T > s + t \mid T > s) = P(T > t)\).)

Solutions

Solution

B1. (a) Gaussian — cell diameters are continuous and roughly symmetric; mean and variance are separate. (b) Poisson — reads are counts; across technical replicates the rate is essentially fixed, so variance \(\approx\) mean is plausible. (c) Exponential — a time between events in a stream (spikes), continuous and non-negative. (d) Negative binomial — reads are counts, but biological replicates (different mice) vary in their true rate, so the data are overdispersed (variance > mean).

B2. With \(\lambda = 2\): (a) \(P(0) \approx 0.135\), \(P(1) \approx 0.271\), \(P(2) \approx 0.271\). One and two reads are the most likely counts (equally likely), and about one sample in seven has no read at all. (b) \(P(X \ge 1) = 1 - P(0) \approx 1 - 0.135 = 0.865\). (c) \(P(X \le 2) \approx 0.135 + 0.271 + 0.271 = 0.677\): about two samples in three. (d) (optional) \(P(0) = \dfrac{2^0 e^{-2}}{0!} = e^{-2} \approx 0.135\), \(P(1) = \dfrac{2^1 e^{-2}}{1!} = 2e^{-2} \approx 0.271\), \(P(2) = \dfrac{2^2 e^{-2}}{2!} = 2e^{-2} \approx 0.271\).

B3. For Poisson\((\lambda)\) the mean and variance are both \(\lambda\). The colleague’s variance (\(\approx 210\)) is about ten times the mean (\(\approx 20\)), so \(\text{Var} \gg \text{mean}\): the data are overdispersed and Poisson is not adequate — it would badly understate the noise. The discrepancy signals extra variation in the rate between samples (biological variability). They should use the negative binomial. (Here \(\mu + \phi\mu^2 = 210\) with \(\mu = 20\) gives \(\phi \approx 0.475\).)

B4. Rate \(\lambda = 0.5\) per Mb. (a) Expected waiting distance \(= 1/\lambda = 1/0.5 = **2\) Mb**. (b) $P() = $ dpois(0, 1) \(\approx 0.37\). “No mutation in the next 2 Mb” and “the gap to the next mutation is longer than 2 Mb” are the same event, read once as a count (zero events in the window) and once as a gap, so they must have the same probability. (Optional: the exponential route gives \(P(T > 2) = e^{-0.5 \cdot 2} = e^{-1} \approx 0.368\), the Poisson route \(P(0) = e^{-1}\) too. E5 and E6 show both in the demo: 36% of windows empty, 38% of gaps longer than 2 Mb.)

B5. \(\text{Var} = \mu + \phi\mu^2\), \(\mu = 50\). (a) \(\phi = 0\): \(\text{Var} = 50 + 0 = 50\); variance equals the mean, so this is the Poisson. (b) \(\phi = 0.5\): \(\text{Var} = 50 + 0.5 \cdot 50^2 = 50 + 0.5 \cdot 2500 = 50 + 1250 = **1300\)** — far above the mean. (c) The Poisson test assumes variance \(= 50\) but the truth is \(1300\); assuming too little noise makes the test overconfident, so p-values are too small. That inflates false positives (Type I errors) — you call noise as differential expression.

B6. (a) False. A Gaussian still allows negative and fractional values and, more importantly, assumes variance is independent of the mean; for counts the variance grows with the mean, and biological counts are overdispersed. (For very large means a Gaussian approximation can be workable after care with the variance, but “just use Gaussian” is not a fix.) (b) True. Poisson forces \(\text{Var} = \text{mean}\), so a mean-variance plot across genes should scatter around the line \(y = x\). Systematic points above \(y = x\) are the signature of overdispersion. (c) False. The exponential is defined only for \(t \ge 0\); waiting times cannot be negative.

B7. Exponential dwell time, mean \(4\) s. (a) \(\lambda = 1/\text{mean} = 1/4 = 0.25\text{ s}^{-1}\). (b) Equally likely. The exponential is memoryless: having already stayed 4 s tells you nothing about the remaining dwell time, so the protein “does not age”. E6 shows it: the gaps that are already longer than 2 Mb have, on average, as much left (1.95 Mb) as a fresh gap (2.00 Mb). (c) (optional) \(P(\text{dwell} > 4) = e^{-0.25 \cdot 4} = e^{-1} \approx 0.368\), and by memorylessness \(P(T > 4 + 4 \mid T > 4) = P(T > 4) = e^{-1} \approx 0.368\), the same number.

R1.

dpois(0:4, 2)        # 0.1353 0.2707 0.2707 0.1804 0.0902  -> the B2 table
1 - dpois(0, 2)      # 0.8647               -> matches P(X>=1)
ppois(2, 2)          # 0.6767               -> matches P(X<=2)

R2.

x <- rpois(1e5, 20)
mean(x); var(x)                       # ~20, ~20  (variance = mean)

y <- rnbinom(1e5, mu = 20, size = 1 / 0.25)  # Var = 20 + 0.25 * 20^2 = 20 + 100 = 120
mean(y); var(y)                       # ~20, ~120

The Poisson gives variance \(\approx 20\); the negative binomial with \(\phi = 0.25\) gives variance \(\approx 120\). Neither exactly matches the colleague’s variance of \(210\) — that needs more dispersion, \(\phi \approx 0.48\) (B3, E4). Either way, the overdispersed NB, not the Poisson, is the family that can reproduce a variance far above the mean.

R3.

pexp(2, rate = 0.5, lower.tail = FALSE)   # 0.3679  (exponential survival, e^{-1})
dpois(0, 0.5 * 2)         # 0.3679  (Poisson zero-count over the 2-Mb window)

Both equal \(e^{-1} \approx 0.368\), confirming the two views of the same Poisson process agree.

Where this leads

  • Bayesian vs frequentist: the error model is the likelihood — the term that multiplies the prior to give the posterior. There you used it in Bayes’ theorem; today you chose it.
  • Hierarchical models: technical and biological variation stack, the same idea as Poisson counting noise plus the extra spread of the negative binomial.
  • Single-cell RNA-seq: data & QC: the same kind of counts, now per cell, with many zeros.
  • Genomics tools: DESeq2 (which you ran in Bulk RNA-seq with DESeq2) and edgeR are negative-binomial models with shared dispersion estimation across genes — the production-grade version of the mean-variance reasoning practised here.
