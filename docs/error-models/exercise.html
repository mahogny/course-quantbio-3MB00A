Exercise: error models — exponential & Poisson

Goal

By the end of this exercise you should be able to:

  • explain what an error model (likelihood) is and why every analysis assumes one,
  • match a data type to the right distribution — Gaussian, Poisson, exponential, negative binomial,
  • use the mean = variance identity of the Poisson by hand and as a diagnostic,
  • compute simple Poisson and exponential probabilities on toy examples,
  • explain overdispersion and why RNA-seq counts need the negative binomial,
  • explain, concretely, why the wrong error model misleads (inflated false positives).

This is a concept day. Spend most of your time reasoning about which distribution fits and why; the R section is there to confirm your hand answers and build intuition by simulation.

Background

A measurement is signal plus noise. The error model is the probability distribution you assume the data came from given the truth; read as a function of the parameters, it is the likelihood (the same likelihood that feeds Bayes’ theorem in the prior, likelihood and posterior unit).

The four distributions of this unit:

Data type Distribution Mean Variance
Continuous, symmetric Gaussian \(\mathcal{N}(\mu,\sigma^2)\) \(\mu\) \(\sigma^2\) (independent of \(\mu\))
Counts (idealised) Poisson\((\lambda)\) \(\lambda\) \(\lambda\)
Waiting times / gaps Exponential\((\lambda)\) \(1/\lambda\) \(1/\lambda^2\)
Overdispersed counts Negative binomial\((\mu,\phi)\) \(\mu\) \(\mu + \phi\mu^2\)

Key formulas:

\[P_{\text{Pois}}(X=k) = \frac{\lambda^k e^{-\lambda}}{k!}, \qquad f_{\text{Exp}}(t) = \lambda e^{-\lambda t}, \qquad P_{\text{Exp}}(T > t) = e^{-\lambda t}.\]

Poisson process: one stream of random events at rate \(\lambda\). The number in a fixed window is Poisson\((\lambda)\); the waiting time to the next event is Exponential\((\lambda)\).

Overdispersion: real biological counts have variance > mean, because the true rate varies between replicates. The negative binomial adds a dispersion parameter to absorb this (\(\phi = 0\) recovers Poisson).

By hand

B1. For each dataset, name the error model you would use and give the one-line reason: (a) cell diameters in micrometres; (b) reads mapped to a gene across 5 technical replicates; (c) time between neuronal spikes; (d) reads per gene across 5 biological replicates (different mice).

B2. A gene has an average of \(\lambda = 2\) reads per sample under a Poisson model. Compute \(P(X=0)\), \(P(X=1)\), and \(P(X=2)\) by hand. What is \(P(X \ge 1)\)?

B3. For a Poisson\((\lambda)\), state the mean and the variance. A colleague reports count data with sample mean \(\approx 20\) and sample variance \(\approx 210\). Is Poisson an adequate error model? What does the discrepancy tell you, and which distribution should they use instead?

B4. Mutations occur along a genome at rate \(\lambda = 0.5\) per megabase, modelled as a Poisson process. (a) What is the expected waiting distance to the next mutation? (b) What is \(P(\text{no mutation in the next 2 Mb})\)? (Two equivalent routes: the exponential survival \(e^{-\lambda t}\), or the Poisson probability of zero events in a window of length 2.)

B5. A negative binomial has \(\text{Var}(X) = \mu + \phi\mu^2\). For \(\mu = 50\): (a) what is the variance when \(\phi = 0\), and what distribution is that? (b) what is the variance when \(\phi = 0.5\)? (c) A Poisson-based test analyses these \(\phi = 0.5\) data. Will its p-values be too large or too small, and does that inflate false positives or false negatives?

B6. True or false, with one line of reasoning each: (a) “A Gaussian is fine for counts as long as the mean is large.” (b) “If counts are Poisson, plotting sample variance against sample mean across many genes should fall near the line \(y = x\).” (c) “The exponential distribution can produce negative waiting times.”

B7. (waiting-time flavour) A DNA-binding protein has a dwell time on its site that is exponential with mean \(4\) seconds. (a) What is the rate \(\lambda\)? (b) What is \(P(\text{dwell} > 4\text{ s})\)? (c) Given it has already stayed 4 s, what is the probability it stays at least 4 s more — and what property does your answer illustrate?

Explore

Do B3 and B5 by hand first. The demo draws 1000 counts from a negative binomial with mean \(\mu\) and dispersion \(\phi\), in this unit’s convention \(\text{Var}(X) = \mu + \phi\mu^2\). The bars are the simulated counts, the red dots the NB probabilities, and the dashed blue outline the Poisson with the same mean.

E1. At \(\mu = 50\), read the theoretical variance for \(\phi = 0.5\), then click Poisson. Do the numbers match your B5 answers? What happens to the histogram and the dashed outline when \(\phi = 0\)?

E2. Back at \(\phi = 0.5\), click draw again a few times. Compare the sample mean and sample variance with theory. Which of the two jumps around more?

E3. Click low mean (\(\mu = 3\)), then step through Poisson, mild overdispersion and strong (many zeros). How many of the 1000 cells have a zero count in each case? What happens to the mean?

E4. Set \(\phi = 0.1\) and move \(\mu\) through 5, 50 and 500. Read var/mean each time (tick mean–variance plot to see it on log axes). Then find the \(\phi\) whose variance at \(\mu = 20\) is closest to the colleague’s 210 in B3.

Solutions

E1. \(\phi = 0.5\): \(50 + 0.5\cdot 50^2 = 1300\). \(\phi = 0\): variance \(= 50\), var/mean \(= 1\): the Poisson (B5a–b). At \(\phi = 0\) the histogram falls on the dashed Poisson outline.

E2. The first draw gives a sample mean of 50.0 and a sample variance of about 1390 (theory 50 and 1300); further draws (about 1280, 1200, 1250) move by a hundred or more, while the sample means stay within about 1 of 50. Variances are harder to estimate than means, especially for overdispersed counts, which is why tools like DESeq2 share dispersion information across genes.

E3. With \(\mu = 3\) (first draw): Poisson gives 49 zeros (theory \(1000\,e^{-3} \approx 50\)); mild overdispersion (\(\phi = 0.1\)) 69; strong (\(\phi = 2\)) 368, more than a third of all cells (theory \(1000\,(1+\phi\mu)^{-1/\phi} \approx 378\)). The theoretical mean stays at 3 throughout (the sample means stay close to it): the NB moves probability both to zero and to a long right tail.

E4. With \(\phi = 0.1\): var/mean \(= 1 + \phi\mu\) is 1.5 at \(\mu = 5\), 6 at \(\mu = 50\) and 51 at \(\mu = 500\); on log axes the NB curve peels away from the line var \(= \mu\) as \(\mu\) grows. At \(\mu = 20\), \(210 = 20 + 400\,\phi\) gives \(\phi \approx 0.48\); the slider’s \(\phi = 0.5\) (variance 220) is closest, so the data in B3 are clearly overdispersed.

In R

Use R to confirm your hand answers and to see the distributions. These are static illustrations; run them in your own session.

Poisson probabilities and the mean = variance identity

lambda <- 2
dpois(0:3, lambda)          # P(X=0..3);  P(0)=e^-2 ~ 0.135
1 - dpois(0, lambda)        # P(X>=1) = 1 - P(0)

x <- rpois(1e5, lambda)     # simulate
mean(x); var(x)             # both ~ 2  ->  mean = variance

Exponential waiting times

lambda <- 0.5               # events per Mb
pexp(2, rate = lambda, lower.tail = FALSE)  # P(T > 2) = e^{-1} ~ 0.368
mean(rexp(1e5, rate = lambda))  # ~ 2 = 1/lambda

# same process, counted per 2-Mb window: Poisson(lambda * 2) = Poisson(1)
dpois(0, lambda * 2)        # P(0 events in 2 Mb) = e^{-1}  -> matches

Poisson vs negative binomial: same mean, different spread

mu <- 50
mean(rpois(1e5, mu));            var(rpois(1e5, mu))            # ~50, ~50
# R uses 'size' = 1/phi;  Var = mu + mu^2/size
mean(rnbinom(1e5, mu = mu, size = 2)); var(rnbinom(1e5, mu = mu, size = 2))  # ~50, >>50

Tasks

R1. Confirm your B2 hand probabilities with dpois(0:2, 2), and check 1 - dpois(0, 2) against your \(P(X \ge 1)\).

R2. For B3, simulate x <- rpois(1e5, 20) and report mean(x) and var(x). Then simulate overdispersed counts with rnbinom(1e5, mu = 20, size = 4) and report its variance. Which one resembles the colleague’s data (mean \(\approx 20\), variance \(\approx 210\))? (Recall \(\text{Var} = \mu + \mu^2/\text{size}\).)

R3. For B4, verify both routes agree: pexp(2, rate = 0.5, lower.tail = FALSE) (exponential survival) and dpois(0, 0.5 * 2) (Poisson zero-count). They should give the same number.

Hints

Hint 1 — picking the distribution (B1, B6)

Ask two questions in order. First: continuous or a count? Continuous symmetric measurements \(\to\) Gaussian; continuous non-negative waiting times \(\to\) exponential; counts \(\to\) Poisson or negative binomial. Second, for counts: technical/idealised or biological replicates? Biological replicates are almost always overdispersed (variance > mean) \(\to\) negative binomial.

Hint 2 — Poisson probabilities (B2)

Use \(P(X=k) = \dfrac{\lambda^k e^{-\lambda}}{k!}\) with \(\lambda = 2\). Note \(0! = 1! = 1\) and \(2! = 2\). For “at least one”, use the complement: \(P(X \ge 1) = 1 - P(X = 0) = 1 - e^{-\lambda}\). See dpois.

Hint 3 — the mean = variance diagnostic (B3)

Poisson forces \(\text{Var} = \text{mean}\). If the sample variance is much bigger than the sample mean, the data are overdispersed and Poisson understates the noise. The fix is the negative binomial, whose variance \(\mu + \phi\mu^2\) can exceed the mean.

Hint 4 — exponential vs Poisson for the same process (B4)

For a Poisson process at rate \(\lambda\): the count in a window of length \(t\) is Poisson\((\lambda t)\), and the waiting distance to the next event is Exponential\((\lambda)\). “No event in length \(t\)” can be read either as the exponential survival \(P(T > t) = e^{-\lambda t}\) or as the Poisson zero-count \(P(X=0) = e^{-\lambda t}\) over that window — same number. See pexp and dpois.

Hint 5 — negative binomial variance (B5)

Plug into \(\text{Var} = \mu + \phi\mu^2\). In R the parameter is size \(= 1/\phi\), so \(\text{Var} = \mu + \mu^2/\text{size}\). A test that assumes less variance than the truth makes p-values too small (overconfident), which produces false positives. See rnbinom.

Hint 6 — memorylessness (B7)

Mean of an exponential is \(1/\lambda\), so \(\lambda = 1/\text{mean}\). The survival function is \(P(T > t) = e^{-\lambda t}\). Memorylessness means \(P(T > s + t \mid T > s) = P(T > t)\) — the past waiting does not matter, so the “already waited 4 s” answer equals the plain \(P(T > 4)\).

Solutions

Solution

B1. (a) Gaussian — cell diameters are continuous and roughly symmetric; mean and variance are separate. (b) Poisson — reads are counts; across technical replicates the rate is essentially fixed, so variance \(\approx\) mean is plausible. (c) Exponential — a time between events in a stream (spikes), continuous and non-negative. (d) Negative binomial — reads are counts, but biological replicates (different mice) vary in their true rate, so the data are overdispersed (variance > mean).

B2. With \(\lambda = 2\): \[P(0) = \frac{2^0 e^{-2}}{0!} = e^{-2} \approx 0.135, \quad P(1) = \frac{2^1 e^{-2}}{1!} = 2e^{-2} \approx 0.271, \quad P(2) = \frac{2^2 e^{-2}}{2!} = 2e^{-2} \approx 0.271.\] \(P(X \ge 1) = 1 - P(0) = 1 - e^{-2} \approx 0.865\).

B3. For Poisson\((\lambda)\) the mean and variance are both \(\lambda\). The colleague’s variance (\(\approx 210\)) is about ten times the mean (\(\approx 20\)), so \(\text{Var} \gg \text{mean}\): the data are overdispersed and Poisson is not adequate — it would badly understate the noise. The discrepancy signals extra variation in the rate between samples (biological variability). They should use the negative binomial. (Here \(\mu + \phi\mu^2 = 210\) with \(\mu = 20\) gives \(\phi \approx 0.475\), i.e. size \(= 1/\phi \approx 2.1\).)

B4. Rate \(\lambda = 0.5\) per Mb. (a) Expected waiting distance \(= 1/\lambda = 1/0.5 = **2\) Mb. (b) \(P(\text{no mutation in 2 Mb})\). Exponential route: \(P(T > 2) = e^{-\lambda \cdot 2} = e^{-0.5 \cdot 2} = e^{-1} \approx 0.368\). Poisson route: events in a 2-Mb window are Poisson\((\lambda \cdot 2) = \text{Poisson}(1)\), so \(P(0) = \frac{1^0 e^{-1}}{0!} = e^{-1} \approx 0.368\). Same answer**, as they must be for one Poisson process.

B5. \(\text{Var} = \mu + \phi\mu^2\), \(\mu = 50\). (a) \(\phi = 0\): \(\text{Var} = 50 + 0 = 50\); variance equals the mean, so this is the Poisson. (b) \(\phi = 0.5\): \(\text{Var} = 50 + 0.5 \cdot 50^2 = 50 + 0.5 \cdot 2500 = 50 + 1250 = **1300\)** — far above the mean. (c) The Poisson test assumes variance \(= 50\) but the truth is \(1300\); assuming too little noise makes the test overconfident, so p-values are too small. That inflates false positives (Type I errors) — you call noise as differential expression.

B6. (a) False. A Gaussian still allows negative and fractional values and, more importantly, assumes variance is independent of the mean; for counts the variance grows with the mean, and biological counts are overdispersed. (For very large means a Gaussian approximation can be workable after care with the variance, but “just use Gaussian” is not a fix.) (b) True. Poisson forces \(\text{Var} = \text{mean}\), so a mean-variance plot across genes should scatter around the line \(y = x\). Systematic points above \(y = x\) are the signature of overdispersion. (c) False. The exponential is defined only for \(t \ge 0\); waiting times cannot be negative.

B7. Exponential dwell time, mean \(4\) s. (a) \(\lambda = 1/\text{mean} = 1/4 = 0.25\text{ s}^{-1}\). (b) \(P(\text{dwell} > 4) = e^{-\lambda \cdot 4} = e^{-0.25 \cdot 4} = e^{-1} \approx 0.368\). (c) By memorylessness, \(P(T > 4 + 4 \mid T > 4) = P(T > 4) = e^{-1} \approx 0.368\) — the same as (b). The protein “does not age”: having already stayed 4 s tells you nothing about the remaining dwell time.

R1.

dpois(0:2, 2)        # 0.1353 0.2707 0.2707  -> matches B2
1 - dpois(0, 2)      # 0.8647               -> matches P(X>=1)

R2.

x <- rpois(1e5, 20)
mean(x); var(x)                       # ~20, ~20  (variance = mean)

y <- rnbinom(1e5, mu = 20, size = 4)  # Var = 20 + 20^2/4 = 20 + 100 = 120
mean(y); var(y)                       # ~20, ~120

The Poisson gives variance \(\approx 20\); the negative binomial with size = 4 gives variance \(\approx 120\). Neither exactly matches the colleague’s variance of \(210\) — that needs a smaller size (more dispersion): size \(= \mu^2/(\text{Var}-\mu) = 400/190 \approx 2.1\). Either way, the overdispersed NB, not the Poisson, is the family that can reproduce a variance far above the mean.

R3.

pexp(2, rate = 0.5, lower.tail = FALSE)   # 0.3679  (exponential survival, e^{-1})
dpois(0, 0.5 * 2)         # 0.3679  (Poisson zero-count over the 2-Mb window)

Both equal \(e^{-1} \approx 0.368\), confirming the two views of the same Poisson process agree.

Where this leads

  • Prior, likelihood and posterior: the error model is the likelihood — the term that multiplies the prior to give the Bayesian posterior. Today you chose it; there you use it in Bayes’ theorem.
  • Poisson and negative binomial distributions: a fuller treatment of count distributions and overdispersion, the statistical foundation of RNA-seq differential expression.
  • Genomics tools: DESeq2 and edgeR are negative-binomial models with shared dispersion estimation across genes — the production-grade version of the mean-variance reasoning practised here.
